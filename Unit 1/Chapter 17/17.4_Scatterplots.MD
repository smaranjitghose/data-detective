## Two measurements per record, one point each

A scatter plot puts one variable on each axis and draws a point per record. It is the standard way to look at a relationship between two numeric columns, and it is the chart that would have prevented several of the errors earlier in this course.

```python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv('penguins.csv')

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 5))
ax.scatter(df['flipper_length_mm'], df['body_mass_g'], alpha=0.6)
ax.set_xlabel('Flipper length (mm)')
ax.set_ylabel('Body mass (g)')
```

342 points rising from lower left to upper right. Flipper length runs from about 172 to 231 millimetres, body mass from 2,700 to 6,300 grams, and the two correlate at 0.871, which appears as a fairly tight upward band.

## Colouring by a third variable

```python
for species, group in df.groupby('species'):
    ax.scatter(group['flipper_length_mm'], group['body_mass_g'],
               label=species, alpha=0.7)
ax.legend()
```

Looping over the groups and calling `scatter` once per group gives each its own colour and a legend entry.

The picture changes meaningfully. What looked like one band is two clusters: Adelies and Chinstraps overlapping in the lower left, Gentoos separated in the upper right. The overall correlation is partly a species difference rather than a within-species relationship, which is the same finding the bill length and depth reversal produced.

Adding a third variable by colour is the most useful single move in exploratory plotting, and it is why plotting by group is worth the extra loop.

## Overplotting

At a few hundred points a scatter plot shows structure. At fifty thousand it shows a solid block, and the dense regions look identical to the sparse ones.

| Fix | What it does | Cost |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| `alpha=0.1` | Overlaps darken, revealing density | Individual points fade |
| `s=2` | Smaller markers leave gaps visible | Harder to see isolated points |
| Sample the data | Plot 5,000 of the 50,000 | You may miss rare cases |
| Hexagonal binning | Counts per cell, drawn as colour | No longer individual points |

`alpha` is the first thing to reach for and the cheapest. Setting it to 0.6, as above, already helps at 342 points where several birds share the same flipper length.

## Marker size as a fourth variable

```python
ax.scatter(x, y, s=df['bill_length_mm'] * 3)
```

Size can carry a further variable, and it sits low on the encoding ranking from the principles unit. Readers judge area poorly and inconsistently.

Use it for a rough sense of magnitude, never for a comparison that matters, and be aware that `s` is measured in points squared, so doubling the value does not double the visual size.

## Adding a reference line

```python
import numpy as np
slope, intercept = np.polyfit(df['flipper_length_mm'].dropna(),
                              df['body_mass_g'].dropna(), 1)
xs = np.array([172, 231])
ax.plot(xs, slope * xs + intercept, color='black', linewidth=1)
```

A fitted straight line makes the trend explicit and makes departures from it visible.

It also invites over-reading. A line through two clusters describes the gap between the clusters as much as any relationship within them, which is precisely what the pooled correlation did. Fit within groups if the groups are the unit you care about.

> A scatter plot is where a correlation coefficient becomes checkable. The number said 0.871; the picture says whether that came from one relationship or from two separated groups.

## What to look for

Five things, and all of them appeared earlier in the course as statistical findings.

The direction and rough strength of the relationship. Whether it is straight or curved, since a correlation coefficient cannot tell you. Clusters, which mean a grouping variable. Points isolated from the cloud, which are outliers in combination even when neither column flags them alone. And a fan shape, where spread grows with the value, which undermines any single measure of spread.

## Practise the third variable

Take two numeric columns from any dataset and plot them plainly.

Then find a categorical column and redraw with one colour per category.

If the shape changes, as it does with the penguin measurements, you have found a grouping variable that any pooled statistic on those two columns would have misrepresented.
