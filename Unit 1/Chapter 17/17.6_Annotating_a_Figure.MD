## Everything around the data

A chart with no labels is a shape. The reader cannot tell what is measured, in what units, or what the point is, and every second spent working that out is a second not spent on the finding.

```python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv('penguins.csv')
means = df.groupby('species')['body_mass_g'].mean()

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 4))
ax.bar(means.index, means.values)
ax.set_ylim(0, 6000)
ax.set_title('Gentoos are about 1.4 kg heavier than the other two species')
ax.set_xlabel('Species')
ax.set_ylabel('Mean body mass (g)')
```

Three elements added, and the chart now states a finding rather than presenting three bars.

## The title carries the argument

The most common title is a restatement of the axes: "Body mass by species". The axes already say that.

A title that states the finding turns the chart into evidence for a claim, which is what an explanatory chart is for. "Gentoos are about 1.4 kg heavier" tells the reader what to see, and the bars confirm it.

For exploratory work the descriptive title is fine, because the audience is you and you know what you plotted.

## Axis labels need units

`Mean body mass (g)` rather than `body_mass_g`. The column name is a variable name; the label is for a reader who has never seen the file.

Units belong in the label whenever they exist. A y axis reading "Revenue" is ambiguous between rupees, lakhs, and crores, and readers will assume whichever is wrong.

## Legends

```python
ax.legend(loc='upper left', frameon=False)
```

A legend appears only when you call `legend()`, and only entries with a `label` are listed.

Two improvements are worth knowing. `loc` places it, and `'best'` lets Matplotlib choose a spot that avoids the data. `frameon=False` removes the box, which is usually visual noise.

For line charts with few series, labelling the lines directly is better than a legend, because it removes the back-and-forth between the line and the key:

```python
ax.text(x_last, y_last, ' Gentoo', va='center')
```

## Annotating a specific point

```python
ax.annotate('heaviest bird',
            xy=(231, 6300), xytext=(200, 6000),
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle='->'))
```

`xy` is the point being annotated and `xytext` is where the text sits. An arrow connects them.

Use this for the one or two points that carry the story: the outlier you are discussing, the month a policy changed, the value the reader should look at. Annotating five things annotates nothing.

## Reference lines

```python
ax.axhline(df['body_mass_g'].mean(), color='grey', linestyle='--', linewidth=1)
ax.axvline(pd.Timestamp('2016-01-01'), color='grey', linestyle=':')
```

A horizontal or vertical line gives the reader something to compare against: an overall average, a target, a threshold, a date when something changed.

Keep them visually quiet. A dashed grey line reads as context; a thick red one competes with the data.

## Gridlines and spines

```python
ax.grid(axis='y', alpha=0.3)
ax.spines['top'].set_visible(False)
ax.spines['right'].set_visible(False)
```

Gridlines help when readers need to estimate values, and only on the axis being measured. Both axes gridded on a bar chart is twice as much ink for no extra information.

Spines are the box around the plot. Removing the top and right ones is a common cleanup that makes the chart lighter without losing anything.

> Every element on a chart either helps the reader answer the question or competes with the elements that do. Adding annotation is subtraction wearing different clothes.

## The order that avoids rework

Plot the data, set the limits, then label. Annotating before the limits are settled means repositioning text afterwards, since `xytext` is given in data coordinates and moves when the axis moves.

## Practise the title

Take three charts you have made and rewrite each title to state the finding rather than describe the axes.

If you cannot write such a title for one of them, that chart does not yet have a finding, and it belongs in the exploratory pile rather than the report.

Then add units to every axis label and check that a reader outside your field would understand them without asking.
