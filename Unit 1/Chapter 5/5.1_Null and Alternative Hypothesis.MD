## Starting from the assumption that nothing is going on

A criminal trial does not begin by weighing whether the accused is guilty or innocent as equally likely stories. It begins by assuming innocence, and the prosecution has to produce enough evidence to overturn that assumption. If the evidence is weak, the accused walks free, which is not the same as the court declaring them innocent.

Hypothesis testing works the same way, and the parallel is worth holding onto, because almost every misunderstanding in this unit comes from forgetting it.

## The two statements

Every test starts by writing down two competing statements about a population.

The null hypothesis, written $H_0$, says nothing interesting is happening: no difference between the groups, no relationship between the variables, no effect from the treatment. This is the assumption the test starts from.

The alternative hypothesis, written $H_1$ or $H_a$, says something is happening. It is what you suspect, and it is what the data has to argue for.

Take a real question from the Palmer file. Adelie and Chinstrap penguins both have bills; do the two species differ in bill length?

| | Statement |
| ----- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| $H_0$ | Adelie and Chinstrap penguins have the same mean bill length |
| $H_1$ | Adelie and Chinstrap penguins have different mean bill lengths |

The file records 151 Adelie bills averaging 38.79 mm and 68 Chinstrap bills averaging 48.83 mm, a difference of 10.04 mm. The test asks how likely a gap that large would be if the two species really had identical bills and this file was just an unlucky draw.

## Why the null gets the benefit of the doubt

The asymmetry is deliberate. Chance alone produces differences between groups, so the interesting question is never whether a difference exists in your sample, it is whether the difference is larger than chance comfortably explains.

Starting from "no effect" gives you something specific to compute against. If the species have identical bills, the mathematics of sampling tells you how much two sample means should typically differ, and you can compare your observed gap to that expectation. Starting from "there is an effect" gives you nothing to compute, because an effect of unspecified size predicts nothing in particular.

> The null hypothesis is not what you believe. It is the position you are trying to make untenable.

## Directional and non-directional alternatives

The alternative can be written in two ways, and the choice must be made before seeing the data.

A two-sided alternative says the two means differ, without saying which is larger. A one-sided alternative says one specific group is larger.

| | $H_1$ | Use when |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Two-sided | The means differ | You have no prior direction in mind |
| One-sided | Chinstrap bills are longer | Theory or prior work predicts the direction |

One-sided tests find smaller effects more easily, which is exactly why they must be chosen in advance. Looking at the data, noticing which group came out higher, and then declaring a one-sided alternative in that direction is a way of manufacturing evidence, and it is common enough to have caused a replication crisis in several fields.

## What the two possible conclusions actually mean

A test ends in one of two ways, and only one of them has a clean interpretation.

Reject $H_0$ means the data would be surprising if nothing were going on, so the assumption of no difference is hard to maintain.

Fail to reject $H_0$ means the data is consistent with nothing going on. It does not mean nothing is going on. A small sample, a noisy measurement, or a real but modest effect all produce this outcome.

| Phrase you hear | What it means | What it does not mean |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| We rejected the null | The data is hard to explain by chance | The alternative is proven |
| We failed to reject | The data does not rule out chance | The null is true |
| The result was significant | The gap was larger than chance easily explains | The gap is large or important |
| No significant difference | We could not detect one | The groups are the same |

The last row causes real damage. "No significant difference between the two treatments" reported from a study of thirty patients often means the study was too small to see anything, and it gets read as evidence the treatments are equivalent.

## Writing hypotheses that can be tested

Three properties make a pair of hypotheses usable.

They concern a population, not your sample. Whether the 151 Adelies in the file have longer bills than the 68 Chinstraps is a question of arithmetic you can settle exactly. Whether the species differ is a question about birds you never measured, and that is what a test addresses.

They are stated before the data is examined. This is the same discipline as the exploratory and confirmatory distinction, and it exists for the same reason.

They are specific enough to compute against. "Something interesting is happening with the penguins" cannot be tested. "The two species have the same mean bill length" can.

## Practise writing the pair

Take three questions from your own world: whether students who attend morning labs score higher, whether one hostel's mess wastes more food, whether a website change increased signups.

For each, write $H_0$ and $H_1$ explicitly, decide before writing whether the alternative is one-sided or two-sided, and say why.

Then write the sentence you would use to report a failure to reject, in words a non-statistician would read correctly. If your sentence sounds like proof that nothing is happening, rewrite it. That single habit prevents the most damaging misreading in this whole unit.
