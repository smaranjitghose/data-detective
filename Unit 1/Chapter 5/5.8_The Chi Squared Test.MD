## The test that assumes you already know the spread

The t-test exists because you rarely know how variable a population is, so you estimate it from your sample. The z-test is what you would use if you did know.

That sounds like a technicality and it explains why the z-test appears constantly in textbooks and rarely in practice. Knowing a population's standard deviation while not knowing its mean is an unusual situation.

It remains worth understanding, because the z-test is the simpler case that the t-test generalises, and because one common application, comparing proportions, uses it directly.

## The statistic

$$z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$$

Here $\bar{x}$ is the sample mean, $\mu_0$ the value stated by the null hypothesis, $\sigma$ the known population standard deviation, and $n$ the sample size. The denominator is the standard error from the central limit theorem.

The result says how many standard errors your sample mean sits from the hypothesised value. Because the sampling distribution of the mean is normal, that distance converts directly into a probability using the same coverage figures as the empirical rule.

| $|z|$ | Roughly how often chance produces this or more | Two-sided p-value |
| ---: | --------------------------------------------- | ----------------: |
| 1.00 | About once in three | 0.317 |
| 1.96 | About once in twenty | 0.050 |
| 2.58 | About once in a hundred | 0.010 |
| 3.29 | About once in a thousand | 0.001 |

The value 1.96 is the one worth remembering. It is where the familiar 95% threshold comes from, and it is why confidence intervals are so often built as the estimate plus or minus about two standard errors.

## z or t

| | z-test | t-test |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Population $\sigma$ | Known | Unknown, estimated from data |
| Distribution used | Normal | t distribution |
| Sample size | Any, given known $\sigma$ | Any, adjusts for small samples |
| How often applicable | Rarely | Almost always |

The t distribution has heavier tails than the normal, which is the mathematical expression of an honest concession: when you estimate the spread from a small sample, your estimate might be off, so extreme results should not surprise you as much.

As $n$ grows the two converge. Beyond a few hundred observations they agree to more decimal places than anyone reports, which is why the choice between them only matters for small samples.

> Use the z-test when the spread is actually known, which usually means a long-established measurement process. Use the t-test the rest of the time, which is nearly always.

## Where it applies: proportions

Proportions are the case where the z-test earns its keep, because the null hypothesis fixes the spread automatically.

If you hypothesise that a coin is fair, you have specified the probability of heads as 0.5, and that single number determines the variability of the count as well as its centre. Nothing has to be estimated.

The same holds for any yes-or-no outcome. Testing whether the completion rate of a form differs from a stated 40%, or whether two versions of a page convert at the same rate, uses a z-test on proportions rather than a t-test.

This is the version you will meet in practice, usually inside a tool that reports the result of an A/B test without naming the test it ran.

## The other place z appears

The same arithmetic, applied to a single observation rather than a sample mean, gives a standardised score:

$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$

Here $x$ is one value, $\mu$ the mean, and $\sigma$ the standard deviation. The result says how many standard deviations that observation sits from the centre.

This is a description rather than a test. It is how values measured in different units become comparable: a bill length and a body mass both convert to a number of standard deviations. It also underlies one common approach to spotting unusual observations, which is treated later in the course.

## Conditions for the proportion version

Two requirements, and both are easy to check.

Observations must be independent. Each visitor's outcome must not depend on any other's, which fails if the same person is counted twice or if people influence each other.

Both expected counts must be reasonably large. The usual guidance is at least ten expected successes and ten expected failures. With smaller counts the normal approximation is poor and exact methods are appropriate instead.

A test of a 2% conversion rate on 200 visitors expects four conversions, which fails that check comfortably, and the reported p-value should not be trusted.

## Practise the standardisation

Take two measurements of yourself in different units: your height in centimetres and your marks in one subject.

Find the mean and standard deviation of each in a group you belong to, then convert both of your values to standardised scores. The two numbers are now on the same scale and can be compared, which the raw values could never be.

Then check the coverage table above. A score beyond 1.96 puts you in the outer 5% of that group, which is a claim about rarity rather than about quality, and keeping those two ideas separate is most of what standardised scores are good for.
