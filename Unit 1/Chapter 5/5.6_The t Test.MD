## Comparing two means when you do not know the true spread

Adelie penguins in the Palmer file have a mean bill length of 38.79 mm across 151 birds. Chinstraps average 48.83 mm across 68 birds. The gap is 10.04 mm.

Two samples from identical populations would not produce exactly equal means either, so the question is whether a gap this size is more than sampling noise comfortably explains.

The t-test answers that, and it is the workhorse of applied statistics because the situation it handles, comparing two group means without knowing the population's true variability, is the situation you are almost always in.

## The idea behind the statistic

The test compares the difference you observed against the amount of difference that sampling alone tends to produce.

$$t = \frac{\text{difference between the group means}}{\text{standard error of that difference}}$$

The numerator is what you found. The denominator is how much the difference would typically wobble if you drew the samples again, which depends on how variable each group is and how large the samples are.

A large $t$ means the observed gap dwarfs the expected wobble. For the penguin bills, $t = -21.86$, meaning the difference is roughly twenty-two times larger than the noise. The corresponding p-value is around $3 \times 10^{-41}$.

The negative sign only records which group was subtracted from which. Its size is what matters.

## Three kinds of t-test

| Type | What it compares | Example |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| One-sample | One group's mean against a fixed value | Is mean delivery time different from 30 minutes? |
| Independent | Two separate groups' means | Adelie against Chinstrap bill length |
| Paired | Two measurements on the same subjects | Marks before and after a tutorial programme |

Choosing between the last two is not optional. A paired test uses the fact that each pair of measurements comes from the same person or object, which removes person-to-person variation from the comparison and makes the test far more sensitive.

Analysing before-and-after data with an independent test throws that away and can easily miss a real effect.

## The equal variance question

The independent t-test comes in two versions, differing in whether they assume the two groups have equal variability.

Student's original version assumes they do. Welch's version does not, and it adjusts the calculation accordingly.

Welch's is the better default. It performs almost identically when the variances are equal and considerably better when they are not, and you rarely know in advance which situation you are in. The penguin comparison above used Welch's, with group standard deviations of 2.66 mm and 3.34 mm.

> If you are choosing between the two versions on the basis of a preliminary test for equal variances, you are running two tests to answer one question. Use Welch's and move on.

## What the test assumes

Three assumptions, in decreasing order of how much they matter.

Independence of observations comes first, and violating it is serious and uncorrectable. If the same penguin were measured twice, or if students in a study copied each other's answers, the test's arithmetic is simply wrong.

Approximate normality within each group comes second, and it matters mainly for small samples. With groups of 151 and 68, the central limit theorem does the necessary work regardless of the underlying shape.

Similar variances comes third, and Welch's version removes the requirement.

## Significance is not size

The bill length result is significant beyond any threshold anyone uses. That fact alone does not say the difference matters.

The 95% confidence interval for the difference runs from 9.13 mm to 10.95 mm. That is the informative statement: the gap is around 10 mm, and the estimate is precise. A biologist can judge whether 10 mm of bill separates two species meaningfully.

Compare it against another pair from the same file. Adelie penguins average 3,700.7 g and Chinstraps 3,733.1 g in body mass, a gap of 32 g. That test returns $t = -0.54$ and a p-value of 0.588, nowhere near significance.

So the two species have clearly different bills and indistinguishable body masses, in the same file, with the same sample sizes. Which is a reminder that "are these groups different" is not a question with one answer; it depends entirely on what you measure.

## Reporting it properly

Four numbers make a t-test result usable: the two group means, the difference between them, a confidence interval on that difference, and the p-value. Sample sizes belong there too.

A report giving only "p < 0.05" is nearly useless, because the reader cannot tell whether the effect was 10 mm or a tenth of a millimetre.

## Practise on paired and unpaired data

Find two comparisons you can make with data you have: marks in two subjects for the same students, and marks in one subject for two different sections.

The first is paired and the second is not. Write down which test applies to each and why, then compute both group means and their difference.

Then ask the more useful question before running anything: how large would the difference need to be for you to do something differently? Answering that first stops the p-value from deciding what counts as important.
