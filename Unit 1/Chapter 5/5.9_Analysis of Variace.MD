## Comparing three groups without running three tests

The Palmer file holds three species. Their mean body masses are 3,700.7 g for Adelie, 3,733.1 g for Chinstrap, and 5,076.0 g for Gentoo.

The obvious approach is three t-tests: Adelie against Chinstrap, Adelie against Gentoo, Chinstrap against Gentoo. That approach has a problem, and the problem grows quickly with the number of groups.

## Why repeated tests fail

Each test at $\alpha = 0.05$ carries a 5% chance of a false alarm when nothing is going on. Run several and those chances accumulate.

| Groups | Pairwise tests | Chance of at least one false alarm |
| -----: | -------------: | ---------------------------------: |
| 3 | 3 | About 14% |
| 4 | 6 | About 26% |
| 5 | 10 | About 40% |
| 10 | 45 | About 90% |

With ten groups you are almost guaranteed to find a significant difference somewhere even if all ten populations are identical. This is the multiple comparisons problem, and analysis of variance is the standard response to it: one test, one p-value, for the whole set of groups.

## What the test asks

| | Statement |
| ----- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| $H_0$ | All group means are equal |
| $H_1$ | At least one group mean differs from the others |

The alternative is deliberately vague. A significant result says the groups are not all the same, without saying which one stands apart.

## The idea behind the name

The test compares two kinds of variation.

Variation between groups measures how far the group means sit from the overall mean. Variation within groups measures how much individuals scatter around their own group's mean.

$$F = \frac{\text{variation between groups}}{\text{variation within groups}}$$

If the groups are alike, both quantities estimate the same underlying variability and $F$ lands near 1. If the group means are far apart relative to the scatter inside each group, $F$ grows.

For body mass across the three penguin species:

```
F = 343.6,  p = 2.89e-82
```

An $F$ of 343.6 means the separation between species is hundreds of times larger than the scatter within them. Looking at the means explains why: Gentoos sit roughly 1,350 g above the other two, while the standard deviations within each species run between 384 g and 504 g.

> Analysis of variance is a question about means, answered by comparing variances. The name describes the method, not the question.

## The follow-up the test cannot do

A significant $F$ says at least one group differs. Finding out which requires further comparisons, and this is where the penguin data becomes instructive.

Comparing Adelie against Chinstrap body mass alone gives $t = -0.54$ and $p = 0.588$. Those two species are indistinguishable in mass. The entire ANOVA result comes from Gentoos.

So the honest summary is not "the three species differ in body mass". It is that Gentoos are much heavier than the other two, which are not detectably different from each other.

Follow-up comparisons after a significant ANOVA are usually adjusted, so that the multiple-comparisons problem does not re-enter through the back door. Several adjustment schemes exist; all of them make each individual comparison more demanding to compensate for the number being made.

## What it assumes

| Assumption | How much it matters |
| --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Observations are independent | Critical, and cannot be repaired |
| Roughly normal within each group | Matters mainly for small groups |
| Similar variability across groups | Matters when group sizes are also unequal |

The third assumption is the one worth checking here. Group standard deviations of 458.6, 384.3, and 504.1 are close enough not to cause concern. Had one group been three times as variable as another, with very unequal sample sizes, a version of the test that does not assume equal variances would be appropriate.

## When ANOVA is the wrong tool

Three situations call for something else.

Only two groups, in which case a t-test answers the same question more directly and reports the difference rather than a ratio.

Repeated measurements on the same subjects, such as the same students tested three times, which need a version that accounts for the pairing.

Groups defined by two factors at once, such as species and sex together, where a two-factor version can also examine whether the effect of one depends on the other.

## Reporting it usefully

An $F$ statistic and a p-value are the least informative part of the result. What a reader needs is the group means, the group sizes, the spread within each group, and which specific groups differ.

For the penguins: three species, means of 3,700.7, 3,733.1, and 5,076.0 grams, with Gentoos separated from both others and the first two indistinguishable. The $F$ of 343.6 is a footnote to that sentence rather than a substitute for it.

## Practise on three groups you have

Find a numeric measurement across three or more groups: marks by section, commute times by mode of transport, spending by day of week.

Write the group means and sizes first. Then predict, before computing anything, whether one group stands apart or whether they all differ.

Then check your prediction against the individual comparisons. The common finding is the one the penguins show: a single group drives the result, and the summary sentence most people would write from a significant ANOVA alone would have been wrong.
