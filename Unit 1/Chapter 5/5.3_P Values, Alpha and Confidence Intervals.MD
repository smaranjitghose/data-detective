## What the number actually measures

A test on the Palmer file comparing Adelie and Chinstrap bill lengths returns a p-value of about 0.000000000000000000000000000000000000000029.

Almost everyone reads that as "the probability the species are the same". That reading is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that changes conclusions.

## The definition, stated carefully

The p-value is the probability of observing data at least as extreme as yours, assuming the null hypothesis is true.

Every word in that sentence is load-bearing. It is a statement about data, computed under an assumption. It is not a statement about the assumption.

For the penguins: if Adelie and Chinstrap bills really were identical in the wider population, a difference of 10.04 mm between samples of 151 and 68 birds would be extraordinarily unlikely. That is what the tiny number says.

| The p-value is | The p-value is not |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ |
| The chance of this data if $H_0$ holds | The chance $H_0$ is true |
| A measure of surprise under an assumption | A measure of effect size |
| Sensitive to sample size | An indicator of practical importance |
| Computed from your data | A property of the world |

The third row explains a great deal of published nonsense. Feed enough data into any test and trivial differences become significant, because significance answers "could chance produce this" and chance gets ruled out easily when samples are large.

## Alpha, and where the line sits

The p-value is a continuous measure of surprise. The decision to act on it needs a threshold, and that threshold is $\alpha$, chosen before the test.

If $p < \alpha$, reject the null. Otherwise, fail to reject.

The convention of $\alpha = 0.05$ is a convention and nothing more. It means accepting a one in twenty chance of a false alarm when the null is true, and it made sense in the agricultural experiments where it originated. Whether it makes sense for your decision is your call, made on the grounds of which error costs more.

> A p-value of 0.049 and one of 0.051 are practically identical pieces of evidence. Only the threshold makes them look like opposite conclusions.

Treating 0.05 as a cliff edge produces two bad habits: reporting results as though 0.049 settled something, and discarding results at 0.06 as though nothing was found. Reporting the actual p-value and letting the reader judge is better practice than reporting whether it cleared a bar.

## Confidence intervals say more

A p-value answers one question: could chance explain this. A confidence interval answers a more useful one: how large is the effect, and how precisely do we know it.

For the bill length comparison, the 95% confidence interval on the difference between Adelie and Chinstrap means runs from 9.13 mm to 10.95 mm.

That single line does everything the p-value did and more. It excludes zero, so the test would reject the null. It says the difference is around 10 mm rather than merely non-zero. And its narrowness says the estimate is precise.

| What you want to know | p-value | Confidence interval |
| -------------------------------- | ------- | ------------------- |
| Could chance explain this? | Yes | Yes, by whether it contains zero |
| How large is the effect? | No | Yes |
| How precise is the estimate? | No | Yes, by its width |
| Is the effect worth acting on? | No | You can judge |

A 95% interval means that if the study were repeated many times, about 95% of the intervals constructed this way would contain the true value. It does not mean there is a 95% probability the true value lies in this particular interval, which is the same category of misreading as the p-value error.

## Reading widths

The width of an interval carries information that a significance verdict throws away.

A narrow interval excluding zero means a well-estimated real effect. A wide interval excluding zero means a real effect of poorly known size. A narrow interval containing zero is informative: it says that if an effect exists, it is small. A wide interval containing zero means the study learned very little.

That third case is the one significance testing handles worst. "No significant difference" is reported identically whether the interval was tight around zero or spanned everything from a large negative to a large positive effect.

## Practices that corrupt the number

Three habits invalidate a p-value, and all three are common.

Running many tests and reporting the significant ones. With twenty tests at $\alpha = 0.05$, one false alarm is expected even when nothing is going on anywhere.

Collecting data until the result crosses the threshold. Watching a p-value as data accumulates and stopping when it dips below 0.05 guarantees eventual significance regardless of the truth.

Choosing the hypothesis after seeing the data. The p-value assumes the question was fixed in advance, and the arithmetic silently breaks when it was not.

## Practise reading real reports

Find three reported results with p-values: a news story about a study, a paper in your field, a product experiment write-up.

For each, write what the p-value is actually claiming, in the careful phrasing above. Then check whether the effect size is reported anywhere, and whether an interval is given.

You will find that many reports give only the p-value, which tells a reader that chance was ruled out and nothing at all about whether the finding matters.
