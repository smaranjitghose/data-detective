## Comparing two versions without fooling yourself

A college's placement portal has a low completion rate: many students start filling in their profile and abandon it halfway. Someone proposes a shorter form.

The obvious move is to switch to the new form and compare next month's completion rate against last month's. That comparison is close to worthless, because everything else changed too. Placement season shifted, a reminder email went out, and exams finished.

An A/B test removes the confusion by running both versions at the same time, on comparable groups, with people assigned at random.

## What randomisation buys

Each visitor is assigned to version A or version B by a random draw. Nothing about the person influences which they see.

That single act handles a problem no amount of later analysis solves. The two groups end up similar in every respect, including respects you never thought to measure and could not measure if you had. Motivation, prior familiarity, device, time of day, and the hundred other things that affect whether someone finishes a form get distributed evenly across both groups.

| Comparison method | What differs between the groups |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| This month against last month | The version, and the season, and everything else |
| Volunteers against everyone else | The version, and whatever makes someone volunteer |
| Randomly assigned A against B | The version, and chance alone |

Only the last row supports a causal claim. That is the point of the design.

## Setting it up before it runs

Four decisions must be made in advance, and writing them down is what separates a test from a fishing expedition.

* The metric. One primary measure, decided first, such as the proportion of started forms that get completed.
* The hypotheses. $H_0$ says the completion rate is the same for both versions; $H_1$ says it differs.
* The threshold and the sample size. How much evidence you need, and how many visitors it takes to detect an effect worth caring about.
* The stopping point. A date or a visitor count, fixed in advance.

The last one prevents the commonest failure in commercial A/B testing. Watching results accumulate and stopping when the difference looks good guarantees you will eventually see a difference, whether or not one exists.

> An A/B test that is stopped when it looks convincing is not a test. It is a search for a moment that flatters the new version.

## Reading the result

Suppose 4,000 students see the old form and 38% complete it, while 4,000 see the new form and 42% complete it.

Two questions follow, in order. Could chance produce a four point gap between two groups of this size? And is four points worth the cost of rebuilding the form?

The first is what a test answers. The second is a judgement no statistic can make for you, and it depends on how many extra completed profiles four points represents and what the rebuild costs.

| Outcome | What it supports |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Large gap, tight interval | Ship the change |
| Small gap, tight interval | The versions are close to equivalent; decide on other grounds |
| Large gap, wide interval | Promising, and the test was too small to be sure |
| Small gap, wide interval | The test learned almost nothing |

## Where A/B tests go wrong

Peeking is the first and worst, described above.

Testing many things at once is the second. Changing the form's length, its colour, and its wording together tells you the combination worked and nothing about which part did. Testing many variants simultaneously has the multiple comparisons problem: with enough variants, one will look good by chance.

Short-run effects are the third. A novelty change can lift engagement for a week and lose it afterwards, so a test run over three days measures curiosity rather than value.

Contamination is the fourth. If students in the two groups talk to each other, or the same person is assigned differently on their phone and laptop, the groups stop being clean.

And a metric that misses the point is the fifth. Optimising for form completions might succeed by removing fields the placement cell actually needed, which improves the number and damages the process.

## When randomisation is not available

Plenty of important questions cannot be tested this way. You cannot randomly assign students to attend lectures, or households to receive a subsidy, without either practical impossibility or an ethical objection.

The honest response is to say what the comparison can and cannot support. Observational comparisons can be informative, and they cannot rule out that the groups differed in ways connected to the outcome. That limitation should appear in the reporting rather than being quietly dropped.

## Design one on paper

Take a change you could plausibly make at your college: a shorter feedback form, a different notice board location, a reminder message sent at a different time.

Write down the primary metric, the two hypotheses, how you would assign people at random, when you would stop, and what size of difference would be large enough to justify the change.

Then list three things that could contaminate the comparison. That list is usually the most useful part, and it is always longer than people expect.
