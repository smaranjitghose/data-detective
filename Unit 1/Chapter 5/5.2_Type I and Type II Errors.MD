## Two ways to be wrong, and they trade against each other

A smoke alarm can fail in two directions. It can go off when you are making toast, or it can stay silent during a real fire.

Both are errors and they are not equivalent. Making the alarm more sensitive reduces the second failure and increases the first. Making it less sensitive does the reverse. No setting eliminates both, and choosing the setting means deciding which failure you can better afford.

Hypothesis testing has exactly this structure.

## The four outcomes

A test either rejects the null hypothesis or does not, and the null is either true or false. That gives four combinations.

| | $H_0$ is actually true | $H_0$ is actually false |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| You reject $H_0$ | Type I error, a false alarm | Correct: you found a real effect |
| You fail to reject $H_0$ | Correct: no effect, none claimed | Type II error, a missed effect |

A Type I error means claiming something is happening when nothing is. A Type II error means missing something that is there.

The probability of a Type I error is called $\alpha$, and you choose it before running the test. The conventional choice is 0.05, meaning you accept a one in twenty chance of a false alarm when the null is true.

The probability of a Type II error is called $\beta$. Its complement, $1 - \beta$, is the power of the test: the chance of detecting an effect that exists.

## Which error costs more depends entirely on the situation

There is no universal answer, and pretending otherwise is how the wrong threshold gets chosen.

| Situation | Type I error costs | Type II error costs | Which to guard against |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------ | ---------------------- |
| Approving a new drug | An ineffective drug reaches patients | A working drug is shelved | Type I |
| Screening for a treatable cancer | An anxious patient and further tests | A missed diagnosis | Type II |
| A website button colour test | A pointless change is shipped | A small gain is missed | Neither much |
| Structural safety inspection | An unnecessary repair | A collapse | Type II |

The default $\alpha$ of 0.05 comes from a convention in agricultural research in the 1920s and has nothing to say about your situation. In screening and safety work it is often far too strict; in drug approval it is often too loose.

> Choosing $\alpha$ is a statement about which mistake you would rather make. It is a judgement about consequences, not a statistical calculation.

## What actually controls power

Power is the one you can improve, and four things move it.

Sample size is the main lever. Larger samples make real effects easier to detect, because the natural variation in a sample mean shrinks as the sample grows.

Effect size matters just as much. A 10 mm difference in bill length between Adelie and Chinstrap penguins is enormous relative to the variation within each species, and even a handful of birds from each would reveal it. Drawing six of each at random from the file produced means of 36.57 mm and 48.03 mm, which is already unmistakable.

A difference of half a millimetre would need hundreds of birds to detect at all.

Variability works against you. The noisier the measurement, the harder any effect is to see, which is why careful measurement is a statistical intervention as much as a practical one.

And $\alpha$ itself trades directly: demanding stronger evidence to avoid false alarms makes real effects harder to detect.

| To increase power | Effect on Type I risk |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------- |
| Collect more data | None, and this is why it is the preferred lever |
| Reduce measurement noise | None |
| Raise $\alpha$ from 0.01 to 0.05 | Increases it |
| Study a larger effect | None, but you rarely choose this |

The first two rows are free improvements. The third buys power by accepting more false alarms, which is a trade rather than a gain.

## Underpowered studies, and why they mislead twice

A study too small to detect its effect fails in a way that is worse than simply finding nothing.

It usually reports no significant difference, which readers translate into evidence of no difference. That is the first harm. The second is subtler: on the occasions when a small study does reach significance, it can only do so if the observed effect happened to be large, so the published effects from small studies are systematically overstated.

Both problems are fixed at the design stage, by working out how many observations you need before collecting any. Doing it afterwards tells you only what you already know.

## Reading a result honestly

Two questions convert a test result into something you can use.

If the test found nothing, ask whether it could have found anything. A study of thirty people looking for a modest difference was never going to.

If the test found something, ask how large the effect is, separately from whether it was significant. With enough data, effects too small to matter become statistically detectable, and significance stops being informative on its own.

## Practise the trade-off

Take the smoke alarm and write down its two error rates as you would set them for three places: a kitchen, a hospital ward, and a server room.

Then take a decision you might actually analyse, such as whether to change the mess menu based on a wastage study. Write which error you would rather make and why, then state the $\alpha$ that follows from that answer.

Doing this before you see any data is the entire point. Afterwards, the numbers argue for whichever threshold gives the answer you already like.
