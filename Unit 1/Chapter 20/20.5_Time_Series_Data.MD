## Data indexed by time

Time series carry a property no other data has: the rows are in an order that means something, and the gaps between them matter.

```python
import pandas as pd
a = pd.read_csv('aapl.csv', parse_dates=['Date']).set_index('Date')
len(a)                                    # 506
(a.index.max() - a.index.min()).days      # 730
```

506 records spanning 730 calendar days. The mismatch is the first thing to establish about any time series.

```python
pd.date_range(a.index.min(), a.index.max()).difference(a.index).size      # 225
```

225 calendar days have no row. Most are weekends, as the weekday counts in the earlier unit showed, and some are public holidays.

Whether that matters depends on the question. For a trading analysis the absence is correct. For a question about elapsed time it is a gap you must account for.

## The datetime index

Setting the date as the index unlocks operations that need it.

```python
a['2016-01':'2016-03']       # slice by date string
a.loc['2016-06-15']          # one day
```

String slicing on a datetime index accepts partial dates, so `'2016'` gives the year and `'2016-01'` gives the month. It is the most convenient filtering in pandas and it requires the index to be datetimes rather than text.

## Resampling

```python
a['AAPL.Close'].resample('ME').mean().round(2).head(3)
```

```
2015-02-28    129.70
2015-03-31    125.97
2015-04-30    127.29
```

Resampling regroups the series onto a regular frequency. `ME` is month end; `D`, `W`, `QE`, and `YE` are the other common ones.

The difference from `groupby` on an extracted month matters. Resampling produces a row for every period in the range, including periods with no data at all, which is what you want when an absence is meaningful. Grouping produces rows only for periods that appear.

| Aggregation | Suits |
| ------------- | -------------------------------- |
| `.mean()` | Levels: price, temperature, rate |
| `.sum()` | Flows: sales, visits, rainfall |
| `.last()` | End-of-period snapshots |
| `.count()` | How many observations per period |

Choosing wrongly here is a common error. Summing a temperature gives a meaningless number; averaging daily sales when you want monthly totals understates by a factor of thirty.

## Rolling windows

```python
r = a['AAPL.Close'].rolling(30).mean()
r.first_valid_index()      # 2015-03-30
r.dropna().iloc[0]         # 127.14
```

A rolling mean smooths short-term movement to show the underlying level. The window here is 30 observations, so the first 29 rows have no value and the series starts on 30 March rather than 17 February.

Two things to know. The window counts rows, not days, so on trading data a window of 30 spans about six calendar weeks. And the default aligns the window to the right, meaning each value summarises the preceding 30 observations, which is correct for anything that must not use future information.

## Change over time

```python
a['AAPL.Close'].pct_change().describe()
```

```
mean   0.0002
std    0.0153
min   -0.0657
max    0.0650
```

Daily returns: an average close to zero, a standard deviation of 1.5%, and extremes at roughly plus and minus 6.5%.

`pct_change` and `diff` convert a level series into a change series, which is often the analysable quantity. A price is not comparable across years; a daily return is.

Note what the distribution says. A 6.5% single-day move sits over four standard deviations from the mean, and there are several such days in two years, which is far more than a normal distribution allows. That is the heavy-tailed behaviour the kurtosis topic described.

## Two hazards

Missing periods are the first, and reindexing to a complete range makes them visible:

```python
a.reindex(pd.date_range(a.index.min(), a.index.max()))
```

Every absent day becomes a row of missing values. Whether to fill them forward, interpolate, or leave them depends on whether the absence means "no change" or "not measured".

Time zones are the second. A timestamp without a stated zone is ambiguous, and grouping by day gives different answers in different zones. Server logs are usually UTC while the business question is in local time, and the shift can move events across day boundaries.

> A time series has a shape, a frequency, and a set of gaps. Establishing all three before any analysis prevents most of the mistakes that follow.

## Practise the three checks

Take any dated dataset.

Count the rows and the calendar days spanned, and find the difference. Count observations per weekday and per month, looking for a pattern that reveals the underlying calendar. And reindex to a complete range to see the gaps explicitly.

Then resample to a coarser frequency and choose the aggregation deliberately, saying out loud why a mean or a sum is the right one for that quantity.
