## Categories with too many values to work with

Species has three values. Country has 142. Product code, pin code, and customer ID can have thousands or millions.

A category column with many distinct values breaks the tools built for categories. A bar chart of 142 countries is unreadable, a grouped summary produces 142 rows nobody will read, and colouring by it exhausts every palette.

```python
import plotly.express as px
g = px.data.gapminder()
g['country'].nunique()      # 142
```

## The top-N pattern

The standard response is to keep the largest few and pool the rest.

```python
latest = g[g.year == 2007]
top = latest.nlargest(10, 'pop')['country'].tolist()
latest['grp'] = np.where(latest['country'].isin(top), latest['country'], 'Other')
latest['grp'].nunique()      # 11
```

142 categories become 11. The five largest by population are China at 1.32 billion, India at 1.11 billion, the United States at 301 million, Indonesia at 224 million, and Brazil at 190 million.

The essential follow-up is measuring what you swept into the bucket:

```
share of countries in 'Other':  93.0%
share of population in 'Other': 38.7%
```

Ten countries out of 142 hold 61% of the population. That is a defensible grouping: the ten named countries dominate the quantity being measured, and "Other" is a genuine remainder.

Had the numbers come out the other way, with "Other" holding 80% of the population, the grouping would be hiding the story rather than simplifying it.

> Always report what proportion of the quantity lands in "Other". A bucket holding most of the total is not a simplification, it is a concealment.

## Rolling up to a real hierarchy

Where a natural grouping exists, use it instead of an arbitrary cutoff.

```python
latest.groupby('continent').agg(countries=('country', 'nunique'), pop=('pop', 'sum'))
```

```
           countries         pop  pop_pct
Africa            52   929539692     14.9
Americas          25   898871184     14.4
Asia              33  3811953827     61.0
Europe            30   586098529      9.4
Oceania            2    24549947      0.4
```

Five rows covering everything, with no residual bucket. Continent was already in the data.

Real hierarchies are common: district into state, product into category, pin code into city, department into faculty. When one exists, rolling up is better than top-N because nothing is discarded and every level is meaningful.

## Which approach fits

| Situation | Approach |
| --------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| A few categories dominate the total | Top-N plus Other |
| A natural hierarchy exists | Roll up to the parent level |
| All categories matter but not at once | Filter to a subset per chart |
| The category is an identifier | Do not group it at all |
| Categories are rare events you care about | Keep them, group the common ones |

That last row inverts the usual instinct. For fraud, failure, or complaint analysis, the rare categories are the subject, and pooling them destroys the analysis.

## The memory cost

```python
df['country'].astype('category')
```

High-cardinality text is expensive. The category conversion helps when distinct values are few relative to rows, and helps much less when nearly every row has a distinct value.

For an identifier column with a million distinct values across a million rows, converting to category makes things worse, since it stores the values plus an index. Leave identifiers as text or as integers.

## Rare categories in modelling terms

A category appearing three times in a hundred thousand rows supports no conclusion. Any average computed for it moves entirely with those three rows.

Setting a minimum count before a category is reported separately is worth doing explicitly:

```python
counts = df['city'].value_counts()
keep = counts[counts >= 30].index
df['city_grp'] = np.where(df['city'].isin(keep), df['city'], 'Other')
```

A threshold based on count rather than rank adapts as the data grows, and it states the standard you are applying rather than picking a round number of categories.

## Practise the two approaches

Take a column with more than twenty distinct values.

Apply top-N grouping and report the proportion of rows and of your measure that falls into "Other". Then look for a natural hierarchy in the data and roll up instead.

Compare which summary a reader would find more useful. Where a hierarchy exists it usually wins, and where it does not, the "Other" proportion tells you whether top-N was honest.
