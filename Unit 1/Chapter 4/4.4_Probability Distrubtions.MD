## A single number cannot describe a column

You know the average body mass of the penguins in the Palmer file. That one number is compatible with many different files: one where every bird weighs nearly the same, one where half are light and half are heavy, one where most are light and a few are enormous.

To tell those apart you need to know which values occur and how often each occurs. That description is a distribution, and it is the most useful object in exploratory work.

## What a distribution is

A distribution lists the possible values a variable can take, together with how often each occurs.

For a categorical column it is exactly a count. The species column in the Palmer file has this distribution:

| Species | Count | Proportion |
| --------- | ----: | ---------: |
| Adelie | 152 | 0.442 |
| Chinstrap | 68 | 0.198 |
| Gentoo | 124 | 0.360 |
| Total | 344 | 1.000 |

The proportions sum to 1, because every bird is one of the three. That is the same rule that governs any probability.

For a numeric column the idea is the same, with one complication. Body mass can take any value in a range, so counting how many birds weigh exactly 3,750 grams is not useful; almost every exact value occurs once or not at all. Instead the values get grouped into intervals, and the distribution describes how many fall in each.

## Discrete and continuous, and why they differ

| | Discrete | Continuous |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------- |
| Values | Countable and separate | Any value in a range |
| Example | Number of siblings, penguins per island | Body mass, flipper length, time taken |
| What you can ask | Probability of an exact value | Probability of falling in an interval |
| How it is described | A probability for each value | A curve where area gives probability |

That third row is the one worth pausing on. For a continuous variable, the probability of any single exact value is effectively zero, because there are infinitely many values it could take. Asking for the probability of a bird weighing exactly 3,750.000 grams is not a sensible question. Asking for the probability it weighs between 3,700 and 3,800 grams is.

This is why continuous distributions are drawn as curves, and why the quantity that means something is the area under a stretch of the curve rather than the height at a point.

> For discrete data, probability lives at the values. For continuous data, probability lives in the intervals.

## What to look at in any distribution

Four features describe most of what matters, and they can be read off a distribution before any calculation.

* Where it is centred: the region most values sit around.
* How wide it is: whether values cluster tightly or spread far.
* Whether it is symmetric: whether the two sides mirror each other or one tail runs longer.
* Whether it has one peak or several: one hump, or two humps with a dip between.

The last one is the most informative and the most missed. Body mass across all 344 penguins has two humps rather than one, because Gentoos are substantially heavier than the other two species. The overall average lands in the dip between them, describing a bird that barely exists in the file.

That is the practical reason distributions matter more than summaries. An average reports a location. A distribution tells you whether reporting a location was sensible at all.

## Observed distributions and theoretical ones

There are two things people call a distribution, and conflating them causes confusion later.

The observed distribution is what your data actually contains: 152, 68, and 124 birds, or the counts falling in each mass interval. It is a fact about your file, computed by counting.

A theoretical distribution is a mathematical shape, described by a formula and a few parameters, that data sometimes resembles. The normal curve is the famous one.

| | Observed | Theoretical |
| ------------- | -------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Comes from | Counting your rows | A formula |
| Exact? | Exactly describes your file | Never exactly describes any file |
| Used for | Describing what you have | Reasoning about what you might see |
| Changes when | You get more data | You change its parameters |

Analysis moves between the two constantly. You look at the observed shape, notice it resembles a known theoretical one, and borrow the mathematics that comes with it. The risk is forgetting that the resemblance was approximate, which is where a good deal of misapplied statistics originates.

## Describing a distribution in words

Before any chart, you can characterise a numeric column with three sentences: roughly where the values sit, how far they spread, and whether anything about the shape is unusual.

For the recorded penguin masses, that reads: most birds sit between about 3,500 and 4,500 grams, the lightest and heaviest differ by well over a kilogram, and the values form two clusters rather than one because the species differ in size.

Those three sentences carry more information than any single statistic, and writing them is the fastest way to find out whether an average is going to mislead you.

## Practise on something you generate

Take twenty numbers you can collect quickly: minutes spent commuting each day, the length of the last twenty messages you sent, marks in one subject across a class.

Sort them, group them into four or five intervals, and count how many fall in each. Then write the three sentences.

The interesting part is what the exercise does to your idea of the average. In most real collections you will find either a long tail on one side or two clusters, and in both cases the average alone would have told you something misleading.
