## The same two facts, asked in two directions

Every Gentoo penguin in the Palmer file was observed on Biscoe island. So if you are told a bird is a Gentoo, you know with certainty it came from Biscoe.

Now turn the question around. You are told a bird came from Biscoe. Is it a Gentoo?

Not necessarily. Biscoe holds 168 birds, of which 124 are Gentoo and 44 are Adelie. The probability is 124 out of 168, about 0.74. Confident, but a long way from the certainty running the other way.

Two questions that use the same two facts, and two very different answers. Keeping them apart is the whole skill.

## Writing it down

The probability of $A$ given that $B$ has occurred is written $P(A \mid B)$, read as "the probability of A given B".

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(B)}$$

The vertical bar means "given". The numerator counts the cases where both happen, and the denominator restricts attention to the cases where $B$ happened at all.

That denominator is the entire idea. Conditioning shrinks the world you are counting in.

| Question | Counted out of | Answer |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------- | ---------- |
| $P(\text{Biscoe} \mid \text{Gentoo})$ | The 124 Gentoos | 124/124 = 1.00 |
| $P(\text{Gentoo} \mid \text{Biscoe})$ | The 168 Biscoe birds | 124/168 = 0.74 |
| $P(\text{Gentoo and Biscoe})$ | All 344 birds | 124/344 = 0.36 |

Three different numbers from one pair of columns, and the only thing that changed is what sat in the denominator.

> $P(A \mid B)$ and $P(B \mid A)$ are different questions. Swapping them by accident is the most common probability error there is, and it survives into professional work.

## Why the swap matters outside penguins

Consider a screening test for a condition affecting 1 person in 10,000. The test correctly flags 99% of people who have it, and wrongly flags 1% of people who do not.

Take a population of one million. About 100 people have the condition, and the test catches 99 of them. About 999,900 do not have it, and the test wrongly flags roughly 9,999 of them.

So among everyone who tests positive, 99 plus 9,999 is 10,098 people, and only 99 actually have the condition. That is under 1%.

The test's accuracy, $P(\text{positive} \mid \text{condition})$, is 99%. What a patient wants to know, $P(\text{condition} \mid \text{positive})$, is under 1%. Both numbers are correct. They answer different questions, and the gap comes entirely from how rare the condition is.

## The base rate does the damage

The reason the swapped question gives such a different answer is the size of the two groups being compared. A small error rate applied to an enormous group produces more cases than a large success rate applied to a tiny one.

| Group | Size | Test says positive |
| ------------------ | -------: | -----------------: |
| Has the condition | 100 | 99 |
| Does not have it | 999,900 | 9,999 |

Whenever you reverse a conditional probability, the relative sizes of the two groups have to enter the calculation. Ignoring them is how confident wrong conclusions get made about rare events: fraud, disease, equipment failure, and any other situation where the thing you are hunting is uncommon.

## Independence, restated

Conditional probability gives a cleaner definition of independence than the multiplication rule did. Two events are independent when conditioning changes nothing:

$$P(A \mid B) = P(A)$$

For the penguins, $P(\text{Gentoo}) = 0.36$ while $P(\text{Gentoo} \mid \text{Biscoe}) = 0.74$. Knowing the island doubles your estimate, so the two are strongly dependent.

This is a useful exploratory move in its own right. Compute an overall proportion, then compute it within a group. When the two differ, the grouping column is carrying information about the outcome.

## Where this shows up in analysis

Conditional thinking is what "split by group" means, and you have already been doing it.

The proportion of students failing a paper is one number. The proportion failing among students who attended fewer than half the classes is a conditional probability, and it is the one that supports a decision. Every time you filter a dataset and compute a proportion, you have conditioned on something.

The discipline is to say out loud what you conditioned on, because the number is meaningless without it. A 74% figure with no stated condition invites a reader to attach whichever condition they had in mind.

## Practise the reversal

Take a claim you have heard with the structure "most X are Y" and write both directions explicitly.

Most professional cricketers played age-group cricket. Does that mean most people who played age-group cricket became professionals? Obviously not, and the reason is the same as the medical test: the two groups are wildly different sizes.

Find three such claims this week and write both conditionals for each. Once you start noticing the reversal, you will find it in news reporting almost daily, usually stated as though the two directions were the same sentence.
