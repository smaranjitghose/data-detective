## When the two sides of a distribution do not match

A company reports an average salary of ₹12 lakh. Most employees earn between ₹5 and ₹8 lakh, and a handful of senior people earn several crore.

Nobody lied. The average is arithmetically correct, and it describes almost nobody in the building. The problem is that the distribution has a long tail on one side, and the mean gets pulled towards it while the typical employee stays where they always were.

Skewness measures that asymmetry. Kurtosis measures something related but distinct: how much of the data lives far out in the tails.

## Skewness, and which way it points

A distribution is right-skewed, or positively skewed, when the long tail runs towards high values. It is left-skewed when the tail runs towards low values. Symmetric distributions have neither.

The fastest detector needs no formula at all. Compare the mean against the median.

| Relationship | Shape | Common examples |
| ------------------- | ------------ | ---------------------------------------- |
| Mean > median | Right-skewed | Income, house prices, waiting times, city populations |
| Mean ≈ median | Symmetric | Heights, measurement errors, well-set exams |
| Mean < median | Left-skewed | Age at death, marks on an easy paper, time to complete a simple task |

The reason is the mean's sensitivity. Every value contributes to it, so a few extreme values on one side drag it in that direction. The median counts positions, so it barely moves.

The nine recorded penguin masses give a small demonstration. Their mean is 3,769.44 grams and their median is 3,650 grams. The mean sits higher, which points to a right tail, and looking at the sorted values confirms it: the two heaviest birds, at 4,250 and 4,675 grams, sit much further above the middle than the lightest sits below it.

> The gap between the mean and the median is not an inconvenience to be averaged away. It is a measurement of how lopsided the data is.

## Putting a number on it

Skewness has a formula, expressed in standard deviations from the mean:

$$g_1 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{s}\right)^{3}$$

Here $x_i$ is the $i$th value, $\bar{x}$ the mean, $s$ the standard deviation, and $n$ the number of values. Each deviation is divided by $s$ to remove the units, then cubed.

The cube is what makes it work. Cubing preserves the sign, so values below the mean stay negative, and it amplifies distant values far more than nearby ones. A long right tail therefore produces a large positive total, a long left tail a large negative one, and a symmetric distribution cancels to near zero.

As a rough reading: values near zero mean roughly symmetric, beyond about ±0.5 means noticeably skewed, and beyond ±1 means strongly skewed.

## Kurtosis, and what it is not

Kurtosis is usually described as peakedness, and that description is wrong often enough to be worth correcting. Kurtosis measures tail weight: how much of the data sits far from the centre.

$$g_2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{s}\right)^{4}$$

The same construction with a fourth power. Because the exponent is even, direction disappears and only distance matters, and the fourth power weights distant values enormously.

A normal distribution has a kurtosis of 3, so software often reports excess kurtosis, which subtracts that 3 to put the normal case at zero.

| Excess kurtosis | Tails | What it implies |
| --------------- | -------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Near 0 | Like a normal curve | The empirical rule roughly applies |
| Positive | Heavier than normal | Extreme values occur more often than expected |
| Negative | Lighter than normal | Values cluster, extremes are rare |

Positive excess kurtosis is the one that costs money. Financial returns are the standard case: daily movements look bell-shaped most of the time, and crashes far beyond three standard deviations happen far more often than a normal distribution allows. Models assuming normality underprice exactly the events that matter.

## What to do once you have found it

Detecting skew is only useful if it changes something. It changes three things.

Report the median rather than the mean, or both. Any summary of income, prices, or durations that quotes only a mean is hiding the shape.

Reach for spread measures based on position. The interquartile range describes a skewed distribution honestly where the standard deviation implies a symmetry that is not there.

Reconsider techniques that assume symmetry. The empirical rule and much else besides expect a roughly normal shape, and skewed data violates that quietly rather than loudly.

## A caution about small samples

Both statistics are unstable on small datasets. With nine penguin masses, moving one bird changes the skewness noticeably, so the number is suggestive rather than conclusive.

The mean-versus-median comparison is far more robust and costs nothing. Use it as the everyday check and reserve the formulas for datasets large enough to support them.

## Practise on a bounded quantity

Take twenty values of something that cannot be negative: daily spending, minutes waiting, file sizes on your phone.

Compute the mean and the median, then note which is larger. Then find the largest value and check how many standard deviations above the mean it sits.

Almost anything bounded at zero and unbounded above will come out right-skewed with at least one value beyond two standard deviations. Once you have seen that on your own data, the sight of a lone mean reported for a quantity like income will stop looking neutral.
