## Turning a standard deviation into something you can picture

A standard deviation of 439 grams is a correct answer to a question nobody asked. What people want to know is whether a particular bird is unusual, and a number in grams does not settle that on its own.

The empirical rule converts spread into coverage. For data with a roughly normal shape, it says what fraction of values fall within one, two, and three standard deviations of the mean.

## The rule

| Distance from the mean | Fraction of values inside | Fraction outside |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------: | ---------------: |
| Within 1 standard deviation | About 68% | About 32% |
| Within 2 standard deviations | About 95% | About 5% |
| Within 3 standard deviations | About 99.7% | About 0.3% |

The interval is written $\mu \pm k\sigma$, where $\mu$ is the mean, $\sigma$ is the standard deviation, and $k$ is how many standard deviations you are counting out.

The third row is the one that reframes how you read extremes. Beyond three standard deviations lies roughly 3 values in every 1,000, so in a class of 60 students you would not expect to see one at all.

## Worked on an exam

Take an exam, marked out of 100, with a mean of 60 and a standard deviation of 10. Assume the marks are roughly bell-shaped.

| Interval | Range of marks | Students inside, out of 500 |
| -------- | -------------- | --------------------------: |
| $\mu \pm 1\sigma$ | 50 to 70 | about 340 |
| $\mu \pm 2\sigma$ | 40 to 80 | about 475 |
| $\mu \pm 3\sigma$ | 30 to 90 | about 499 |

A student scoring 82 sits a little beyond two standard deviations, which places them in roughly the top 2.5% of the cohort. A student scoring 95 is beyond three, and in a cohort of 500 you might expect one such student in total.

Notice how much work the standard deviation is doing. With $\sigma = 20$ instead, the same 82 would sit barely one standard deviation out and would be unremarkable.

> The rule converts a distance into a rarity. That conversion is what makes "unusual" a measurable idea rather than an opinion.

## The half that people forget

The percentages describe both tails together. If 5% of values sit outside two standard deviations, then 2.5% sit above and 2.5% below.

Most practical questions are one-sided. Nobody worries about a machine running unusually cold and unusually hot at the same time; they worry about one direction. Halving the outside percentage is the step that gets skipped, and it makes results sound twice as extreme as they are.

## Where the rule stops working

The empirical rule is a property of the normal shape, not of data in general. Applied to data that is not roughly normal, it can be badly wrong.

| Situation | What goes wrong |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Right-skewed data such as income | The lower bound of $\mu - 2\sigma$ can fall below zero, which is impossible |
| Two clusters, as with pooled penguin masses | The mean sits in the gap, so "within one standard deviation" covers a region where few values live |
| Heavy tails | Extreme values occur far more often than 0.3% |
| Small samples | The mean and standard deviation are themselves unstable |

The first row gives a fast diagnostic you can apply anywhere. Compute $\mu - 2\sigma$ for a quantity that cannot be negative. If the answer is negative, the data is not normal, and the rule does not apply.

For a distribution of household incomes with a mean of ₹40,000 and a standard deviation of ₹35,000, two standard deviations below the mean lands at negative ₹30,000. Nobody earns that, and the calculation has just told you the shape is skewed.

## A rougher rule that always holds

When you cannot assume a normal shape, a weaker statement is still available: at least 75% of values fall within two standard deviations of the mean, and at least 89% within three, whatever the shape of the distribution.

Those bounds are much looser than 95% and 99.7%, which is the price of assuming nothing. They are useful as a floor when the shape is unknown or clearly not bell-like.

## Practise on marks you have

Take the marks for one subject across your class, or any twenty numbers you have.

Compute the mean and standard deviation. Count how many values actually fall within one standard deviation of the mean, and convert that to a percentage.

If you land near 68%, the data is behaving roughly normally. If you land well above or below, you have found a shape that is not bell-like, and the more interesting question is which of the failures in the table above explains it.
