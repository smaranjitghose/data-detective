## What a weather app means by forty percent

Your phone says there is a 40% chance of rain this afternoon. It either rains or it does not, so what could that number possibly mean?

Two answers circulate. One says that among all the afternoons in the app's records that looked like this one, rain fell on about four in ten. The other says the forecaster is 40% confident. The first is a statement about counting, and it is the one that underpins everything in this unit.

Probability is a way of putting a number on how often something happens across many repetitions, when any single repetition is unpredictable.

## The vocabulary, on one roll of a die

Three words are needed before anything else, and a single die introduces all of them.

| Term | Meaning | On one roll of a die |
| ------------ | -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Outcome | One thing that can happen | Rolling a 4 |
| Sample space | Every outcome that could happen | {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} |
| Event | A set of outcomes you care about | Rolling an even number: {2, 4, 6} |

The probability of an event is written $P(A)$, where $A$ is the event. When every outcome is equally likely, it is a count divided by a count:

$$P(A) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes in } A}{\text{number of outcomes in the sample space}}$$

Rolling an even number gives 3 favourable outcomes out of 6, so $P(\text{even}) = 3/6 = 0.5$.

That formula only works when the outcomes are equally likely, which is true of fair dice and almost nothing else you will meet in real data.

## Three rules every probability obeys

Whatever the situation, three properties always hold, and they are worth memorising because they catch errors quickly.

* Every probability sits between 0 and 1. A negative probability or one above 1 means the arithmetic went wrong.
* The probabilities of all outcomes in the sample space add to exactly 1. Something must happen.
* If two events cannot both occur, the probability of one or the other is the sum of their probabilities.

A quick check on the third: a die cannot show both a 2 and a 5, so $P(2 \text{ or } 5) = 1/6 + 1/6 = 2/6$. When events can overlap, this simple addition breaks, which is the subject of the next topic.

## Counting, or observing

Real data rarely offers equally likely outcomes, so probability gets estimated from what happened.

The Palmer Station file records 344 penguins: 152 Adelie, 68 Chinstrap, 124 Gentoo. Pick one row at random and the probability it holds a Gentoo is 124 divided by 344, which is 0.3605.

That is the same division as the dice example, but its meaning is different. With a die the probability comes from the structure of the object. With the penguins it comes from counting what was collected, and it describes this file. Whether Gentoos make up 36% of penguins in the archipelago depends on how the birds were chosen, which is a sampling question rather than a probability one.

> A probability computed from data is a proportion. Calling it a probability is a claim that the file resembles the world.

## Where intuition fails

Two mistakes come up constantly, and both are worth meeting now.

The first is expecting short runs to look average. Ten coin tosses producing eight heads feels wrong, and it happens often. Probabilities describe long-run behaviour, and short sequences are lumpy. The correction that people imagine, where a run of heads makes tails more likely next time, does not exist. The coin has no memory.

The second is ignoring how rare the underlying event is. A test that is right 99% of the time sounds conclusive until you apply it to a condition that affects one person in ten thousand, at which point most of the positive results are wrong. That arithmetic needs conditional probability and comes shortly.

## Reading probability statements carefully

| Statement | What it actually claims |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| 40% chance of rain this afternoon | On similar afternoons, rain fell four times in ten |
| The probability of a six is 1/6 | The die's structure gives six equal outcomes |
| 36% of the penguins in this file are Gentoo | A count, describing this file exactly |
| There is a 90% chance this student passes | An estimate from students who resembled them |

The third row is certain and the fourth is a model. Both get written the same way, which is why asking where a probability came from is a habit worth forming.

## Practise on something you can count

Take a repeatable event you have records of: which bus you catch, whether you finish an assignment the day before it is due, how often a particular app crashes.

Write down the sample space, define one event, and estimate its probability by counting. Then ask the harder question: over what set of repetitions does that number apply? Your commute in this semester, or your commute in general?

The second question is where probability stops being arithmetic and starts being an argument about the world, and it never fully goes away.
