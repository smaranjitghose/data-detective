## Adding probabilities without double counting

A class of students takes two electives. Twenty students take robotics, eighteen take data visualisation, and the teacher wants to know how many take at least one of the two.

Adding gives thirty-eight, and thirty-eight is wrong whenever anyone takes both, because those students were counted twice. The correction is to subtract the overlap once.

That single idea, count everything once, is what the probability rules formalise.

## The complement rule

The probability that an event does not happen is one minus the probability that it does.

$$P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$$

Here $A$ is any event. In the Palmer file, the probability a randomly chosen row holds a Gentoo is 0.3605, so the probability it does not is 0.6395.

This looks trivial and it is a workhorse. Questions of the form "at least one" are usually easier as one minus the probability of none.

## The addition rule

For two events $A$ and $B$:

$$P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)$$

The final term removes the double count. When the events cannot both occur, that term is zero and the rule reduces to plain addition.

The penguin file makes this concrete. Counting species against island gives:

| | Biscoe | Dream | Torgersen | Total |
| --------- | -----: | ----: | --------: | ----: |
| Adelie | 44 | 56 | 52 | 152 |
| Chinstrap | 0 | 68 | 0 | 68 |
| Gentoo | 124 | 0 | 0 | 124 |
| Total | 168 | 124 | 52 | 344 |

Take $A$ as "is a Gentoo" and $B$ as "was observed on Biscoe". Then $P(A) = 124/344 = 0.3605$ and $P(B) = 168/344 = 0.4884$. Both happen together for 124 birds, so $P(A \text{ and } B) = 0.3605$.

Applying the rule: $0.3605 + 0.4884 - 0.3605 = 0.4884$.

The answer equals $P(B)$ exactly, and the table shows why. Every Gentoo is on Biscoe, so "Gentoo or Biscoe" describes precisely the birds on Biscoe. Naive addition would have given 0.8489, which is wrong by a wide margin.

## The multiplication rule and independence

Two events are independent when knowing one occurred tells you nothing about whether the other did. For independent events:

$$P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)$$

This is a test as much as a formula. Multiply the two separate probabilities and compare against the actual joint probability. If they match, the events are independent. If not, they are related.

For species and island: $0.3605 \times 0.4884 = 0.1760$. The actual joint probability is 0.3605. They are nowhere near equal, so species and island are strongly related in this file, which is exactly the conclusion the cross-tabulation reached by inspection.

> Independence is not an assumption you make for convenience. It is a claim you can check with one multiplication.

Coin tosses are independent: the first toss changes nothing about the second. Drawing two cards without replacement is not, because the first draw changes what remains.

## Where the rules get misapplied

| Mistake | What it looks like | Why it fails |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Adding overlapping events | 20 + 18 students, ignoring those in both | Some are counted twice |
| Assuming independence to multiply | Multiplying two risks that share a cause | The joint probability is far higher than the product |
| Treating "and" as more likely than "or" | Any "and" exceeding either single probability | An "and" can never exceed either part |
| Forgetting the sample space changed | Applying old probabilities after a condition | The denominator moved |

The third row gives a fast sanity check. If a calculation says the probability of two things happening together is larger than the probability of one of them, something is wrong.

Assuming independence when it does not hold is the most expensive error in this list, and it does real damage outside classrooms. Risks that share a common cause, such as several loans that all depend on the same regional economy, are far more likely to fail together than the multiplication suggests.

## Worked, in one pass

What is the probability a randomly chosen bird is an Adelie or was observed on Torgersen?

From the table, $P(\text{Adelie}) = 152/344 = 0.4419$ and $P(\text{Torgersen}) = 52/344 = 0.1512$. The overlap is 52 birds, so $P(\text{both}) = 0.1512$.

Applying the rule: $0.4419 + 0.1512 - 0.1512 = 0.4419$.

Again the overlap swallows one whole term, because every Torgersen bird is an Adelie. The rules do not fail here, they simply reflect a file where two columns are entangled.

## Practise where the overlap is real

Take a group you know: your class, a club, a WhatsApp group.

Pick two properties that some members share, such as taking a particular elective and living in a hostel. Count the four combinations, arrange them in a table like the one above, and compute the probability of one property or the other.

Then multiply the two separate probabilities and compare against the joint count. Any large gap means the two properties travel together in your group, and working out why is usually more interesting than the arithmetic.
