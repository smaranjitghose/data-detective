## Why averages behave better than the things they average

Waiting times for a service are badly behaved. Most are short, a few are enormous, and the distribution has a long right tail with nothing resembling a bell curve about it.

Now take samples of thirty waiting times and compute the average of each sample. Do it two thousand times and look at the distribution of those two thousand averages.

That second distribution is symmetric, single-peaked, and close to normal, even though the thing being averaged is nothing of the sort. This is the central limit theorem, and it is the reason statistics works at all on messy real data.

## A demonstration

Here is a simulation on a deliberately skewed population of a hundred thousand waiting times, generated with a fixed random seed so the numbers can be reproduced.

The population itself has a mean of 700.7 and a median of 547.0, a gap that confirms a long right tail. Its standard deviation is 501.0.

Now draw two thousand samples at each of four sample sizes, and look at how the sample averages behave:

| Sample size $n$ | Average of the sample means | Spread of the sample means | Predicted spread |
| --------------: | --------------------------: | -------------------------: | ---------------: |
| 1 | 706.9 | 507.9 | 501.0 |
| 5 | 699.7 | 223.6 | 224.1 |
| 30 | 702.1 | 92.2 | 91.5 |
| 100 | 701.0 | 50.0 | 50.1 |

Two things happen as $n$ grows. The averages stay centred on the population mean of 700.7, so nothing drifts. And their spread shrinks steadily, from 508 down to 50.

At $n = 1$ the distribution of "averages" is just the population, skewed and wide. By $n = 30$ it is tight and symmetric.

## What the theorem says

For samples of size $n$ drawn from a population with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, the distribution of the sample mean $\bar{x}$ approaches a normal distribution with:

$$\text{mean} = \mu \qquad \text{standard deviation} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

Here $\mu$ is the population mean, $\sigma$ the population standard deviation, and $n$ the sample size. The standard deviation of the sample mean has its own name, the standard error.

Check it against the table. With $\sigma = 501$ and $n = 30$, the predicted standard error is $501 / \sqrt{30} = 91.5$, and the simulation produced 92.2. At $n = 100$ the prediction is 50.1 against an observed 50.0.

> The population's shape does not have to be normal. The distribution of its sample means becomes normal anyway, provided the sample is large enough.

## The square root, and what it costs

The $\sqrt{n}$ in the denominator has a practical consequence worth internalising early.

To halve the spread of your estimate, you must quadruple your sample. Going from 30 to 120 observations halves the standard error; going from 30 to 60 improves it only by a factor of about 1.4.

| Sample size | Standard error when $\sigma = 501$ |
| ----------: | ---------------------------------: |
| 25 | 100.2 |
| 100 | 50.1 |
| 400 | 25.1 |
| 1600 | 12.5 |

This is why survey costs rise so steeply for modest gains in precision, and why a national poll of two thousand people is not obviously worse than one of five thousand.

## How large is large enough

The usual guidance is thirty, and like most round numbers it hides the real answer, which depends on the shape of the population.

| Population shape | Sample size before means look normal |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------ |
| Already roughly normal | Any size, even 2 or 3 |
| Mildly skewed | Around 15 to 30 |
| Strongly skewed, like waiting times | 30 to 50 |
| Extremely heavy tails, power laws | Hundreds, sometimes never in practice |

That last row is a genuine limitation rather than a technicality. For distributions where one observation can dominate the total, sample means converge extremely slowly, and applying normal-based reasoning to an average of city populations or insurance claims will mislead you regardless of sample size.

## What it does not say

Three misreadings are common and worth naming.

It does not say your data becomes normal as you collect more. The waiting times stay skewed forever. It is the distribution of averages that becomes normal, which is a different object entirely.

It does not say larger samples fix bias. A badly selected sample of ten thousand gives a tightly estimated wrong answer, and the shrinking standard error makes that wrong answer look more credible.

It does not apply to every statistic. The theorem is about means and sums. The maximum of a sample, for instance, behaves quite differently and has its own theory.

## Why it underpins the rest of statistics

Almost every technique for reasoning from a sample to a population relies on knowing how a sample statistic would vary if you repeated the sampling. The central limit theorem supplies that knowledge for means, without requiring you to know the population's shape.

That is what makes it possible to say how far an estimate might plausibly be from the truth, and everything built on that idea depends on the arithmetic demonstrated in the table above.

## Practise the shrinking

Take any collection of at least fifty numbers you have access to, skewed if possible.

Compute its mean. Then draw samples of five, compute the mean of each, and do that twenty times. Note how much those twenty means vary. Repeat with samples of twenty.

The variation between your sample means should drop by roughly half when the sample size quadruples. Watching that happen on numbers you gathered yourself makes the $\sqrt{n}$ stop being a formula and start being a fact about how estimates behave.
