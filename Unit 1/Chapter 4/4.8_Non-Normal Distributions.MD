## Four shapes that are not bell curves

The normal distribution gets most of the attention, and most of the data you will meet does not follow it. Four other shapes turn up constantly, each with a characteristic cause and each demanding a different response.

Recognising which one you are looking at, before computing anything, prevents a large share of the mistakes in exploratory work.

## Uniform: every value equally likely

A uniform distribution has a flat shape. Every value in the range occurs about as often as every other, with no peak and no tails.

Rolling a fair die produces one. So does a well-implemented random number generator. In real datasets a uniform shape is rare and usually suspicious rather than reassuring.

Suspicious because genuine measurements almost never come out flat. A column of ages spread perfectly evenly from 18 to 60, or transaction amounts equally common at every value, usually means something generated the data rather than recorded it: a test fixture left in production, a default filling in blanks, or a system assigning values in rotation.

## Right-skewed: a floor at zero and no ceiling

This is the most common shape in business and social data, and its cause is structural.

Income cannot go below zero and has no upper limit. Neither can waiting time, file size, city population, or rupees spent per customer. The values pile up near the low end and trail off to the right, sometimes very far.

| Quantity | Why it is right-skewed |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Household income | Bounded below at zero, a few very high earners |
| Delivery time | Cannot be negative, occasional long delays |
| Sales per customer | Most buy little, a few buy enormously |
| Time on a webpage | Most leave quickly, some leave the tab open |

The response is consistent: report the median, use the interquartile range for spread, and be suspicious of any technique assuming symmetry.

## Bimodal: two populations in one column

A bimodal distribution has two peaks with a dip between them. It almost always means the file contains two groups that should be described separately.

The Palmer penguin file demonstrates this. Pooling body mass across all 344 birds produces two clusters, because Gentoos are substantially heavier than Adelies and Chinstraps. The overall mean lands in the dip between the humps, describing a bird that scarcely exists.

Split by species and each group looks reasonably bell-shaped. The two-humped shape was never a property of penguins; it was a property of mixing three species in one column.

> A second hump is not a curiosity. It is a grouping column asking to be found.

Common causes: two sexes, two product lines, two sites, weekday and weekend, before and after a policy change. The fix is always the same, which is to find the column that separates them and analyse the groups apart.

## Power law: the tail is the story

A power law distribution has an extremely long right tail, far heavier than an ordinary skewed distribution. A small number of cases account for a large share of the total.

City populations follow this shape. So do book sales, website traffic, wealth, earthquake magnitudes, and word frequencies in any language.

Two properties make power laws distinctive, and both undermine ordinary summaries.

The mean is unstable. Add one more city to a dataset of city populations and the average can jump substantially, because the new city might be Mumbai. In a normal distribution, one extra value barely moves anything.

Extremes are not errors. In a normal distribution a value five standard deviations out is almost certainly a mistake. In a power law it is Tuesday, and removing it as an outlier destroys the phenomenon you were studying.

## Telling them apart

| Shape | Signature | Typical cause | What to report |
| ------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| Normal | Symmetric, one peak | Many small additive effects | Mean and standard deviation |
| Uniform | Flat, no peak | Generated or defaulted data | Investigate before analysing |
| Right-skewed | One peak, long right tail | Floor at zero, no ceiling | Median and interquartile range |
| Bimodal | Two peaks with a dip | Two groups mixed together | Each group separately |
| Power law | A few enormous values dominating | Growth that compounds on success | Median, and the tail explicitly |

The mean-versus-median comparison distinguishes the first three quickly. Bimodality does not show up in either statistic, which is why looking at the distribution itself, rather than at summaries of it, remains necessary.

## Why this matters before any technique

Every summary statistic and most statistical methods carry an assumption about shape. Applying them to the wrong shape produces output that is correct arithmetic and wrong conclusions.

A mean on a power law describes nobody. A standard deviation on a bimodal column measures the distance between two groups rather than the variation within either. The empirical rule on right-skewed income puts a boundary below zero.

None of these produce error messages. They produce numbers, which is worse.

## Practise recognising them

Take three quantities you can gather twenty values of: minutes commuting, rupees spent per day, and messages received per hour.

For each, sort the values, note the mean and the median, and decide which of the five shapes it most resembles.

Then find one dataset where you expect two groups to be mixed, such as commute times on weekdays against weekends, and check whether pooling them produces two humps. That exercise, done once, changes how you read every average you encounter afterwards.
