## How a number becomes something you can see

Every chart converts numbers into visual properties. A bar chart turns a value into a length. A scatter plot turns two values into a position. A heatmap turns a value into a colour.

Those properties are called channels, and they are not interchangeable. People read some of them accurately and others badly, and the difference decides how well a chart works before any question of style arises.

## The channels, ranked by accuracy

| Channel | How accurately it is read | Suits |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Position on a shared scale | Best | The most important comparison |
| Length from a common baseline | Very good | Magnitudes, counts |
| Angle or slope | Moderate | Trends, not precise values |
| Area | Poor | Rough magnitude only |
| Colour intensity | Poor | Patterns, not values |
| Colour hue | Categories only | Group membership |

The ranking comes from experimental work on how well people judge each channel, and it has a direct consequence: put the comparison that matters most on position, and use the weaker channels for context.

This is the reason a scatter plot outperforms a bubble chart, and why a bar chart outperforms a pie chart. Bars use length from a shared baseline; pie slices use angle and area, both of which sit lower on the list.

## Area is worse than people expect

Doubling a circle's radius quadruples its area, and readers judge somewhere between the two, inconsistently.

A bubble chart where one value is four times another may be read as anything from twice to four times as large depending on whether the drawing scaled the radius or the area, and the reader cannot tell which.

Where area is unavoidable, scale by area rather than radius and label the values. Where it is avoidable, use position or length.

## Colour has three separate jobs

Colour is the most misused channel because it does different things depending on the data type.

| Data | Palette | Example |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| Categories, no order | Distinct hues | Species, department, city |
| Ordered values | One hue, light to dark | Counts, rates, magnitudes |
| Values around a midpoint | Two hues meeting in the middle | Change against a baseline, profit and loss |

Using distinct hues for ordered data destroys the ordering, since nothing about red says it is larger than green. Using a light-to-dark ramp for unordered categories implies a ranking that does not exist.

Two further constraints. Around one in twelve men has some form of colour vision deficiency, most commonly red and green, so a red-green scheme is unreadable for a meaningful fraction of any audience. And more than about seven categorical colours exceeds what anyone can match against a legend.

> Colour should carry information the chart could not carry otherwise. Colour applied because a chart looked plain adds a channel that readers will try to interpret.

## The baseline problem

Length is only readable from a common baseline. This is why stacked bar charts fail beyond two categories.

In a stack of four segments, only the bottom one starts at zero. The other three float, so comparing the third segment across bars means comparing lengths that begin at different heights, which people cannot do.

Grouped bars, all starting at zero, allow that comparison. The stack is better only when the total matters more than the parts.

## Redundant encoding

Using two channels for the same variable is not waste. It is insurance.

A line chart distinguishing three series by colour alone becomes unreadable when printed in greyscale, projected badly, or viewed by someone with colour vision deficiency. Distinguishing them by colour and line style survives all three.

The cost is a little visual clutter. The benefit is a chart that works in conditions you did not anticipate.

## What position implies

Position is the strongest channel and it carries an assumption: that the axis is a scale.

Placing categories along an axis in alphabetical order and connecting them with a line implies a progression that does not exist. Ordering the same categories by value, as bars, uses position to carry something real, namely the ranking.

Whenever the horizontal axis is categorical, its order is a decision you are making. Alphabetical is a default, not a neutral one.

## Practise reading the channels

Take three charts from a news site or a report.

For each, write down which variable is on which channel: what is position, what is length, what is colour, what is area.

Then ask whether the most important comparison in the chart is on the strongest available channel. In most published charts it is not, and the mismatch usually explains why the chart is harder to read than it should be.
