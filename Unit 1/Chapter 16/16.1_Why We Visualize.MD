## Four datasets with identical statistics

In 1973 the statistician Francis Anscombe published four small datasets, eleven pairs of numbers each, chosen to make a point that no argument makes as well.

| Set | mean x | variance x | mean y | variance y | correlation | slope | intercept |
| --- | -----: | ---------: | -----: | ---------: | ----------: | ----: | --------: |
| I | 9.0 | 11.0 | 7.5 | 4.127 | 0.816 | 0.5 | 3.0 |
| II | 9.0 | 11.0 | 7.5 | 4.128 | 0.816 | 0.5 | 3.0 |
| III | 9.0 | 11.0 | 7.5 | 4.123 | 0.816 | 0.5 | 3.0 |
| IV | 9.0 | 11.0 | 7.5 | 4.123 | 0.817 | 0.5 | 3.0 |

Every summary statistic agrees to two decimal places. Means, variances, correlation, and the line of best fit are effectively identical.

The four datasets are nothing like each other.

Set I is a scatter of points around a rising line, which is what the statistics suggest. Set II is a clean curve, rising then falling, with no straight-line relationship at all. Set III is a perfect straight line with one point pulled far off it. Set IV has ten points stacked at a single x value and one point far to the right, which is doing all the work: remove it and there is no relationship to measure.

Anyone reading only the statistics would describe all four the same way, and would be wrong about three of them.

## What summaries cannot carry

Every statistic reduces many numbers to one, and the reduction discards a specific kind of information.

| Summary | What it cannot show |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------- |
| Mean | Whether the data is one cluster or two |
| Standard deviation | Whether the spread is symmetric |
| Correlation | Whether the relationship is a straight line |
| Count | Whether the values are evenly distributed |
| Quartiles | Gaps, or the shape between the quartiles |

The course has already met each of these. Body mass across three penguin species has two humps that no mean can express. Bill length and depth correlate negatively overall and positively within every species. Two Chinstraps sit outside their species' fences while the pooled file flags nothing.

Every one of those was found by splitting and recomputing. Looking at the shape finds them in one step.

## What the eye is good at

Vision handles certain judgements faster and more reliably than any calculation.

Groups and clusters, which appear as separated clouds. Trends, seen as a general slope. Outliers, seen as isolated points. Gaps, seen as empty regions where values should be. Changes in spread, seen as a fan opening out.

None of these require a number to be noticed. All of them require several numbers to be established, and some of them, like a gap in the middle of a range, have no standard statistic at all.

> A statistic answers a question you already thought to ask. A picture of the data shows you the question you did not.

## What the eye is bad at

The same speed produces reliable errors, and the second half of this unit is largely about them.

Judging area and angle accurately, which is why pie charts and bubble sizes mislead. Comparing lengths that do not share a baseline. Resisting patterns that are not there, since the visual system finds structure in randomness readily. And ignoring the frame, so an axis choice made by someone else changes what you conclude.

This is why visualisation is a skill rather than a step. The picture can mislead as thoroughly as the summary, and it does so more persuasively because it feels like direct observation.

## Both, in sequence

The working practice is not to choose. Compute the summaries, look at the shape, and treat a disagreement between them as the interesting finding.

A mean far from a median, a correlation that vanishes within groups, an outlier that changes a slope: in each case the number and the picture say different things, and that gap is where the analysis actually is.

## Practise on Anscombe

The four datasets are eleven rows each and available in most statistical software, including seaborn's built-in datasets.

Compute the mean, variance, and correlation for each and confirm they match the table above. Then plot each one and look at how different they are.

Doing both, in that order, on data small enough to read row by row, is the fastest way to stop trusting a correlation coefficient on its own.
