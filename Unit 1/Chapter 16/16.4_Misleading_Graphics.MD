## Correct data, dishonest picture

A chart can use accurate numbers, contain no errors, and still lead every reader to a false conclusion. The mechanism is the frame around the data rather than the data itself.

## The truncated axis

Mean body mass by species: Adelie 3,701 grams, Chinstrap 3,733, Gentoo 5,076.

Drawn as bars from zero, the Gentoo bar is 1.37 times the height of the Adelie bar. That ratio is the ratio in the data.

Start the axis at 3,600 instead, and the bars now show only the part above that line. The Gentoo bar becomes 14.61 times the height of the Adelie bar.

| Axis starts at | Apparent height ratio |
| -------------: | --------------------: |
| 0 | 1.37 |
| 3,600 | 14.61 |

The numbers printed on the chart are unchanged. The visual impression is an order of magnitude different, and readers take the impression rather than the labels.

For bar charts the rule is firm: bars encode length from a baseline, so the baseline must be zero. Truncating a bar chart's axis misrepresents the data by construction.

Line charts are the exception. They encode change rather than magnitude, and forcing a line chart of temperatures or share prices to include zero can compress the variation into a flat line that hides everything. The requirement there is to label the axis clearly so the reader sees the range.

> A bar chart with a non-zero baseline is not a stylistic choice. The length of the bar is the number, and cutting the bar changes the number the reader sees.

## Other frame manipulations

| Technique | What it does | How to spot it |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Truncated axis | Exaggerates small differences | Check where the axis starts |
| Inverted axis | Reverses the apparent trend | Read the axis direction |
| Unequal intervals | Distorts rates of change | Check the spacing of tick labels |
| Dual axes | Manufactures apparent correlation | Two scales on one chart |
| Selective range | Hides the period that contradicts | Ask why it starts where it does |
| Aggregation choice | Hides variation within groups | Ask what the bars are averaging |

The selective range is the hardest to detect, because nothing in the chart is wrong. A series starting in a year that happens to be a low point makes any subsequent rise look dramatic, and the reader has no way to know what came before.

## Chartjunk

Decoration that carries no information competes with the data for attention: 3D effects, gradients, background images, heavy gridlines, drop shadows.

Three-dimensional bar charts are the worst offender because they actively distort. The perspective makes rear bars appear smaller and the depth makes the top of each bar ambiguous, so the reader cannot tell which value the bar reaches.

The test for any element is whether removing it loses information. Gridlines usually survive that test if they are faint; a background photograph never does.

## Aggregation as concealment

The subtlest misleading chart is one that aggregates away the finding.

Three bars of species means are accurate, and they hide that two of the three groups overlap almost completely, that one group has half the sample size of another, and that two Chinstraps sit well outside their species' range.

This is not a trick anyone plays deliberately, which is what makes it common. A bar of a mean is the default output of every reporting tool, and it discards the distribution every time.

## Reading a chart defensively

Five questions, in order, before believing anything.

Where does each axis start, and what are its units? What is the full range of the data, and does the chart show it? What is being averaged, and over how many observations? What would the chart look like with a different but equally defensible choice? And who made it, and what would they prefer you conclude?

That last question is not cynicism. Every chart is made by someone with a point to make, including yours.

## Your own charts

The same techniques appear in honest work by accident. A plotting library that auto-scales axes will truncate a bar chart without asking, and the default is chosen for visual balance rather than for accuracy.

Two habits cover most of it. Set the baseline to zero explicitly on any bar chart, rather than accepting the default. And show the same data two ways before choosing which to publish, because the pair reveals how much your framing is doing.

## Practise the distortion

Take three group means from any dataset and compute the true ratio between the largest and smallest.

Then compute the apparent ratio if the axis started just below the smallest value, as the arithmetic above does.

The gap between those two numbers is what a truncated axis buys, and producing it deliberately once makes it recognisable everywhere afterwards.
