## Matching the chart to the question

Choosing a chart is not a matter of taste. The question and the data types between them narrow the sensible options to one or two, and the remaining choice is about emphasis.

The wrong chart is not merely ugly. A pie chart of twelve categories, or a line chart over unordered categories, actively obstructs the comparison it claims to show.

## Start from the question

| The question | Chart |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| How is one numeric column distributed? | Histogram, or a box plot |
| How do groups compare on one number? | Bar chart, or box plots side by side |
| How do two numeric columns relate? | Scatter plot |
| How does something change over time? | Line chart |
| How often does each category occur? | Bar chart of counts |
| How do two categories occur together? | Grouped bars, or a heatmap |

The distinction that matters most is between comparing categories and showing a trend. Bars compare discrete things; lines imply continuity between points. A line chart over departments suggests that the space between Mechanical and Civil means something, and it does not.

## Then check the data types

The types decide what is even possible.

| Columns you have | Sensible charts |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| One numeric | Histogram, box plot |
| One categorical | Bar chart of counts |
| One numeric, one categorical | Bars, or box plots per group |
| Two numeric | Scatter plot |
| Two numeric, one categorical | Scatter with colour by category |
| Two categorical | Grouped bars, heatmap |
| Numeric over time | Line chart |

Colour, size, and panels extend these to a third or fourth variable. Beyond that, no single chart works, and the honest answer is several charts.

## Distributions deserve more attention than they get

The most common mistake in reporting is a bar chart of group means where a distribution was needed.

Three bars showing Adelie at 3,701 grams, Chinstrap at 3,733, and Gentoo at 5,076 are accurate and hide everything the earlier units established: that the first two groups overlap almost entirely, that each has a spread of around 400 grams, and that one group has 68 members against another's 152.

A box plot per species shows the spread, the overlap, and the outliers in the same space a bar chart uses for three numbers.

> A bar of a mean shows one number and implies certainty. A box plot shows the same number plus everything it is hiding, for the same ink.

## Charts that rarely earn their place

| Chart | Problem | Better |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Pie chart | Angles are hard to compare | Bar chart |
| Donut chart | The same, with the centre removed | Bar chart |
| 3D anything | Perspective distorts the values | The 2D version |
| Dual axes | Two scales invite false correlation | Two charts, aligned |
| Stacked bars beyond two categories | Only the bottom segment has a baseline | Grouped bars |

Pie charts survive one use: showing that a single category dominates a whole. Comparing five similar slices is a job for bars.

Dual-axis charts deserve their reputation. Two series on two scales can be made to appear correlated or uncorrelated by adjusting either scale, and the reader has no way to detect it.

## Volume changes the choice

The right chart at fifty points is often wrong at fifty thousand.

A scatter plot of a few hundred points shows structure. The same plot with a hundred thousand points is a solid block where the dense regions are invisible, and the fixes are transparency, sampling, or a binned summary of density.

Similarly, a bar chart works for a handful of categories and fails at forty. Sorting by value and showing the top ten, with the rest grouped, answers the question the reader actually has.

## The check before you draw

Three questions, in order.

What am I comparing? Not "what does the data look like", but the specific comparison a reader should be able to make.

What is the smallest chart that supports it? Every additional element competes for attention with the one thing the chart exists to show.

Would a table be better? For fewer than about five numbers, usually yes. A chart of three values is a decoration around three values.

## Practise the mapping

Take a dataset and write five questions you could ask of it.

For each, name the chart type and the columns that go on each axis, before drawing anything.

Then find one question in your list that a table would answer better than any chart. There is almost always one, and noticing it is what keeps a report from filling with charts that carry three numbers each.
