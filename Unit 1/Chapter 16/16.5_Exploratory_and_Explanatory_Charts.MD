## Charts for finding, charts for telling

An analyst working through a dataset might produce forty charts in an afternoon. A report from that work contains two.

Those are different kinds of object, built to different standards, and confusing them wastes time in one direction and loses the argument in the other.

## The two kinds

| | Exploratory | Explanatory |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Audience | You | Someone else |
| Purpose | Find out what is there | Show what you found |
| Number produced | Many, quickly | Few, carefully |
| Lifespan | Minutes | The life of the report |
| Labels and titles | Whatever the library defaults to | Written deliberately |
| Time each deserves | Seconds | As long as it takes |
| Success looks like | You noticed something | The reader reaches your conclusion |

The ratio is roughly forty to one, and the effort per chart is inverted.

## Exploratory charts should be cheap

The point of an exploratory chart is to be replaced. You draw a distribution, see it is skewed, split by group, redraw, notice a cluster, split again.

Anything spent on titles, colours, or fonts during that loop is wasted, because the chart exists for thirty seconds. Default settings are correct here.

What matters instead is speed and coverage. Draw the same column several ways, split by every plausible grouping column, and look at each for a few seconds. The charts you throw away are not failures; they are how you established that nothing was there.

## Explanatory charts carry an argument

An explanatory chart has one job: a reader who spends ten seconds on it should reach the conclusion you reached.

That requires decisions the exploratory version never made. Which comparison to foreground, what to leave out, what the title asserts, what the axes are labelled in units the reader knows, and which single element gets emphasis.

The title is the most underused element. A title saying "Body mass by species" describes the axes, which the axes already do. A title saying "Gentoos are 1.4 kilograms heavier than the other two species" states the finding, and everything below it becomes evidence for a claim rather than a picture to interpret.

> An explanatory chart with a descriptive title asks the reader to do the analysis. A title that states the finding turns the chart into support for it.

## What to remove

Explanatory charts get better by subtraction more often than by addition.

Remove gridlines that are not needed to read values. Remove the legend when direct labels on the lines work better. Remove categories that are not part of the comparison. Remove decimal places nobody needs, since 3,701 grams and 3.7 kg carry the same meaning and one is readable.

Every element that stays should be answering the reader's question. Every element that does not is competing with the ones that do.

## The transition between them

The mistake in one direction is polishing exploratory charts, which is slow and produces attachment to findings that have not survived checking.

The mistake in the other direction is publishing an exploratory chart, which is what happens when the last chart from the working session goes into the report unchanged. It carries default titles, a column name on the axis, and every group rather than the two being compared.

A useful discipline: when a finding is settled, rebuild the chart from scratch rather than editing the exploratory one. It takes ten minutes and it forces every choice to be made deliberately.

## What the reader needs that you already know

You have spent a week with the data. The reader has ten seconds and no context.

They do not know what the columns mean, how many observations sit behind each bar, what was excluded, or which comparison is the point. Every one of those has to be visible in the chart or its caption, and every one of them is invisible to you because you already know it.

The check is to show the chart to someone uninvolved and ask what it says. Where they hesitate is where the chart is relying on knowledge you have and they do not.

## Practise the rebuild

Take an exploratory chart you have made and write down, in one sentence, the finding it should communicate.

Then rebuild it from scratch for that sentence. Change the title to state the finding, drop everything not supporting it, label the axes in units a reader knows, and emphasise the one element that carries the point.

Then show both versions to someone and ask what each says. The gap between the two answers is what the rebuild bought.
