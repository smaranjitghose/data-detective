## An analysis is a loop, and it opens with a question

A municipal corporation hands an intern three years of property tax collection records: several CSV files, one per zone, a few hundred thousand rows, and a note saying "see what you can find".

The usual response is to open the largest file, scroll for a while, chart something, and then sit looking at it. The chart is correct. It leads nowhere, because it was not built to answer anything. An hour later there are six of them and no progress.

Nothing is missing here except an order of operations.

## Five stages, each producing what the next one needs

| Stage | What you do | What you finish with | Sign you moved on too early |
| ----------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Question | Write what someone would decide differently depending on the answer | One or two questions in plain words | Charts nobody can interpret |
| Wrangle | Load, combine, and fix what blocks the question | A workable table and a list of its flaws | Numbers that change when you reload |
| Explore | Count, group, and compare, one column then two | Candidates, most of which will not survive | The first pattern becomes the finding |
| Visualise | Build the views that make a pattern visible or kill it | A few charts carrying an argument | Charts made before you know the argument |
| Communicate | State the finding, its size, and what it excludes | Something a decision-maker can use | The audience gets your process, not your answer |

## Writing a question that can be answered

The first stage produces one sentence, and most beginners write it badly. Compare three attempts at the same task:

| Attempt | Problem with it |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ |
| Analyse the property tax data | No decision attached, no way to know when you are done |
| Find interesting patterns in collections | "Interesting" is decided after the fact, so anything qualifies |
| Which zones had collection rates drop sharply in one year, so the recovery team knows where to start? | Names the comparison, the unit, and who acts on it |

The third one immediately decides which files matter, which columns matter, and what an answer will look like. A good test: if someone handed you the answer right now, could you say what would happen next? If not, the question is not finished.

## A single pass, made concrete

Here is what one loop through those five stages actually produces on the tax files.

The question is the one above. Wrangling means loading the zone files, stacking them into one table, and discovering that two zones use a different column name for the assessed value and one stores dates as text. You fix those three things and write down four more oddities you are leaving alone for now.

Exploring means counting properties per zone per year, then collection rates per zone per year, then looking at which zones moved most. Three zones stand out. Two of them turn out to have fewer properties in the later year, which is a data question rather than a collection question.

Visualising means one chart of collection rate over three years for those three zones, which either shows a clear drop or shows you were looking at noise. Communicating means a paragraph naming the one zone that survived, how large the drop was, and the fact that two neighbouring zones have incomplete records so the comparison excludes them.

That is a complete pass, and it is small. The temptation is to do all of it at once for every zone before saying anything.

## The loops are the work

Written as a list this looks like a pipeline you walk through once. Real analysis moves backwards constantly.

* Exploring sends you back to wrangling, because one zone's rows stop halfway through 2023 and you need to know why before any comparison holds.
* Visualising sends you back to exploring, because a chart shows two clumps and nobody knows what separates them.
* Communicating sends you back to the question, because the person who asked says "interesting, but what I need to know is whether it is the same properties every year".

Going backwards is normal. What matters is noticing, and updating the written question when it changes, so the analysis and the question do not quietly drift apart.

> If you cannot say which stage you are in, you are in the wrangling stage, and you have probably been there too long.

## Three stalls that cost the most time

Endless wrangling comes first. There is always one more column to tidy, and cleaning feels productive because progress is visible. Clean what blocks the current question and put the rest on a list. The list itself is useful output; it tells the next person what the data does wrong.

Charting without a question comes second, which is what the intern did in the first hour. A chart made to see what turns up will always turn something up, and you have no basis for judging whether it matters.

The third stall is reporting the process instead of the finding. A write-up that walks through every file loaded and every dead end explored buries the one sentence the reader needed. The exploration was necessary. Most of it does not belong in the output.

## Keeping the loop visible while you work

Two lightweight habits keep a long analysis from turning into a scroll of disconnected cells.

Keep a running list of open questions, at the top of your working file, and add to it whenever something odd appears. Most oddities are not worth chasing now, and writing them down is what lets you not chase them without also forgetting them.

And record the answer next to the question the moment you get it, in one sentence. You will understand a result clearly for about ninety seconds after you read it. What you write in that window is what survives to next week.

## Knowing when a pass is finished

A pass ends when you can state a finding in one sentence, say roughly how large it is, and say what it does not cover. If any of those three is missing you are still exploring, whatever the chart count suggests.

Short passes beat long ones early on. A rough answer to one question, delivered to the person who asked, tells you whether the question was worth asking, and their reaction shapes the next pass better than another day alone with the files would.

Try it on any dataset you have access to. Write one question, spend ninety minutes, and produce three sentences: the finding, its size, and its limits. The discipline is in stopping at ninety minutes, not in what you find.
