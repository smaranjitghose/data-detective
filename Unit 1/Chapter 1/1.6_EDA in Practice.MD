## Real analysis usually starts with a number that moved

Your grocery delivery app promises a slot and mostly keeps it. One Monday, in one city, it stops keeping it. The share of orders delivered on time drops and stays down.

Inside the company, nobody builds anything that Monday. What happens instead is a series of splits.

* Is the drop across the whole city, or two pin codes?
* Every hour, or the evening peak?
* All stores, or the two that switched to a new picking system last week?
* New riders or experienced ones?
* Every order size, or only large baskets?

By the afternoon the drop is usually traced to a corner of the data, and the corner tells the operations team what to change.

> When a number moves, find where the movement is concentrated before explaining why it moved.

Explaining an aggregate produces stories, and stories are impossible to tell apart from guesses. Slicing produces a location, and a location usually makes the cause obvious.

## Why slicing beats theorising

Watch what each split rules out. If the drop appears in every pin code equally, a local road closure is out. If it appears only in the evening peak, a change to the morning shift is out. If it tracks two specific stores, the whole city-level explanation collapses at once.

Five splits can eliminate most of the plausible causes in under an hour, which is faster than any meeting in which people propose explanations from memory. This is the everyday form of the work, and it is why exploration is a job rather than a preliminary.

## The same shape of work, across sectors

The questions differ by industry. The shape does not: someone has a decision to make and a file that might inform it.

| Sector | A question exploration answers | The data behind it | The decision that follows |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Quick commerce | Which stores and hours explain the drop in on-time delivery? | Order timestamps, store and rider logs | Where to add capacity this week |
| Banking | Which transactions look unlike anything this customer has done? | Transaction histories per account | Which alerts a human reviews |
| Public health | Which districts report dengue cases earliest each season? | State surveillance and hospital records | Where prevention resources go first |
| Electricity distribution | Which feeders show consumption that does not match billing? | Meter readings and billing records | Which areas to audit |
| Education | Which subjects lose students between semesters? | Enrolment and result records | Where to place tutorial support |
| Manufacturing | Which shifts and machines produce most of the defects? | Line inspection logs | Which line to stop and service |

None of these is answered by a technique alone. Each begins with someone slicing a dataset along the dimensions that plausibly matter.

## Checking whether a plan is possible at all

The second common use is quieter and arguably more valuable. Before a company commits to something expensive, someone checks whether the data can support it.

A team proposing to predict which customers will leave needs records of customers who have left, a reliable date for when they left, and information recorded before that date. Exploration is how you discover that the system marks accounts closed only when somebody cleans the database twice a year, which makes that date close to useless.

Finding that out in week one costs a week. Finding it out in month four costs the project.

This is why analytics teams treat a data audit as the first real stage of work rather than as preparation for it. The audit often changes the plan and occasionally cancels it, which is the cheapest outcome on offer.

## What the job looks like at entry level

The picture students carry into their first analytics role is usually of building things. The reality of the first year is mostly the two activities above, plus a third: answering questions from people who are not analysts.

A typical week involves several requests of the form "why did this number change", one longer piece of exploration for a decision being made next month, and a recurring report someone needs on Mondays. The skills that make the difference are unglamorous: knowing where the data comes from, noticing when a definition has quietly changed, and being able to say what a number does not cover.

## Three ways this goes wrong inside organisations

Each of these is a people problem wearing a data costume, and none is fixed by better technique.

* Exploration is commissioned after the decision is made, to support it. Enough slices exist that one will agree with whatever was already chosen.
* Dashboards replace looking. A dashboard answers the questions it was built for, and a metric moving for an unanticipated reason shows up as a line going down with no explanation attached.
* Available data is treated as relevant data. Teams explore what their software happens to log, and conclude that what is not logged is not happening.

The last one is the quiet killer. A delivery company has excellent records of completed orders and nothing at all about the customer who opened the app, saw a ninety minute slot, and closed it. Every analysis of that dataset describes people who stayed, and none of it describes the problem the company most wants to solve.

## Practise on a number you can see

Take a metric you have access to: your own weekly screen time, a college's placement percentage, the runs scored by a team across a season.

Write down the splits you would use to locate a change in it. For screen time, that might be by app, by day of week, by hour, by weekday against weekend. Then actually run two of them.

The habit worth building is narrow and portable. When something changes, locate it before you explain it. The pressure runs the other way, because the people asking are in a hurry and an explanation offered from the aggregate is usually accepted. Being the person who can name which two pin codes and which two hours account for most of a drop takes an extra hour, and earns you the next question that matters.
