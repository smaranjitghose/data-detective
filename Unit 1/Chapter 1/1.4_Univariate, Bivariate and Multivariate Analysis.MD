## One column, then two, then the one you forgot

Three questions about your own classroom, in increasing order of difficulty.

How tall is everyone? Do taller students weigh more? And does that second answer still hold if you look at first-years and final-years separately?

Each question needs more of the data than the one before it, and each one can overturn the answer to the one before it. That ladder has names. Climbing it in order, rather than jumping to the top, is most of what careful analysis is.

The penguin measurements from Palmer Station give a file to climb it on. It holds 344 birds of three species, observed on three islands in the Palmer Archipelago, with bill length, bill depth, flipper length, body mass, and sex recorded for each.

## What to ask of a single column

Univariate analysis examines one column and nothing else. Five questions cover almost everything worth knowing:

* What values appear, and how often does each one appear?
* What does a typical value look like?
* What do the extreme values look like, at both ends?
* How many entries are missing?
* Do any values look impossible?

Run those against `species` and the answer is a count: 152 Adelie, 68 Chinstrap, 124 Gentoo. Already this changes how you read everything later. Chinstrap penguins are less than half as numerous as Adelies, so any statement about Chinstraps rests on a fifth of the file.

Run the same questions against `flipper_length_mm` and you learn the range the birds fall in and that a few rows have no measurement at all. You learn nothing about species yet. That is the point of the level.

This is the least glamorous stage and the first one beginners skip, and it is where recording errors announce themselves. A flipper length of 1810 instead of 181 is obvious the moment you look at that column alone. It is invisible in every more sophisticated view built on top of it, and it will quietly distort each one.

| Level | The question it answers | On the penguin file | What it cannot tell you |
| ------------ | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| Univariate | What is inside this column? | 152 Adelie, 68 Chinstrap, 124 Gentoo | Whether species relates to anything |
| Bivariate | Do these two columns move together? | Gentoos have much longer flippers | Whether something else explains it |
| Multivariate | Does that survive once other columns are considered? | Whether island accounts for the gap | Anything about columns you left out |

## Two columns, and the question of together

Bivariate analysis puts two columns side by side and asks whether knowing one tells you anything about the other.

Split flipper length by species and a clear result appears: Gentoo penguins have distinctly longer flippers than Adelie and Chinstrap penguins, far enough apart that flipper length alone separates most Gentoos from the other two species. That sentence is not stored anywhere in the file. It came from pairing one column with another.

The pairing works differently depending on what kinds of columns you have, and it helps to know which of the three situations you are in.

| The pair | How you compare them | What you get out |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| Two measured columns | Look at whether one rises as the other rises | Whether they move together, and which way |
| A measured and a labelled column | Split the measurement by the label and compare groups | Whether the groups differ, and by how much |
| Two labelled columns | Count how often each combination occurs | Whether some combinations are rare or absent |

Flipper length against species is the middle row. Species against island is the bottom row, and that one is about to matter.

## When a third column rewrites the answer

Group the birds by island instead of species. Penguins on Biscoe are larger on average than those on Dream or Torgersen, and it would be natural to write a sentence about island conditions producing bigger birds.

The sentence would be wrong. Count species against island and the reason is immediate:

| | Biscoe | Dream | Torgersen |
| --------- | -----: | ----: | --------: |
| Adelie | 44 | 56 | 52 |
| Chinstrap | 0 | 68 | 0 |
| Gentoo | 124 | 0 | 0 |

Every Gentoo in the file lives on Biscoe, and no Chinstrap does. Biscoe is not an island effect. It is the Gentoo column wearing a different label. Split by species first and the island difference mostly disappears.

Notice also what the zeros mean for any comparison you might want to make. There is no such thing as a Chinstrap on Biscoe in this data, so a question of the form "do Chinstraps grow larger on Biscoe than on Dream" cannot be answered at all. That is not a subtle statistical point. It is four zeros in a table you can read in ten seconds.

> A two column finding is a lead. It becomes a conclusion only after you have asked what else could produce it.

Adding a third column can strengthen a relationship, weaken it, confine it to one group, or dissolve it completely. Which of those happens is not predictable in advance, which is the entire reason for looking.

## Choosing which pairs are worth your time

A file with twenty columns has one hundred and ninety possible pairs, and roughly eleven hundred triples. Looking at all of them is slow, and it is a reliable way to find patterns that are noise.

Three things narrow the field to something sensible. Start from the question, and examine the pairs it names. Add the columns that plausibly explain what you find, which usually means the ones describing how the data was collected: who, where, when, by which instrument. And include any column you suspect is standing in for another, the way island stands in for species here.

That is normally five or six pairs, not a hundred and ninety.

## Where the ladder gets climbed badly

Splitting the data until something looks dramatic is not multivariate analysis. With enough splits, any dataset produces a group where something appears striking, and what you have found is the shape of your own persistence rather than a property of penguins.

The two ordinary failures sit at either end of the ladder. Stopping after one column gives a description that is correct and useless: you know how flipper lengths are spread and nothing about what drives them. Jumping straight to many columns builds conclusions on columns nobody checked, with the 1810 still sitting in there.

There is a third failure that is harder to see. A relationship that holds inside every group can look weaker, or even reverse, when the groups are pooled together, purely because the groups are of different sizes and sit at different levels. When a combined figure and a group-by-group figure disagree, the group-level one is almost always the one to trust.

## Which level answers this?

| The question asked | The level it needs |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------ |
| What is the heaviest bird in the file? | Univariate |
| Do heavier birds have longer flippers? | Bivariate |
| Does that hold separately for each species? | Multivariate |
| How many birds have no sex recorded? | Univariate |
| Are males and females spread evenly across species? | Bivariate |

## Practise on the file itself

Three tasks, in order, on the penguin data.

Count the rows for each island and note how uneven the coverage is. Then compare body mass across the three species and write one sentence describing the difference. Then repeat that comparison separately for males and females, and check whether your sentence still holds.

If your sentence changes at the third step, you have found exactly what this topic is about, and you have found it on your own file rather than in a paragraph telling you it happens.
