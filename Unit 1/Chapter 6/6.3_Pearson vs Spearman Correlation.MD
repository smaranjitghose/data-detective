## When the ranks agree but the values do not

Pearson's coefficient measures how close two variables come to lying on a straight line. Plenty of real relationships are perfectly orderly without being straight, and plenty of real files contain a mistyped value that wrecks the arithmetic.

Spearman's coefficient handles both by throwing away the values and keeping only their order.

## What Spearman does

Replace each column with its ranks. The smallest value becomes 1, the next becomes 2, and so on. Then compute Pearson's correlation on the ranks instead of the original numbers.

That is the whole method. The symbol is $\rho$, sometimes written $r_s$, and like Pearson's it runs from −1 to +1.

Because only order survives, Spearman answers a different question. Pearson asks whether the relationship is close to a straight line. Spearman asks whether one variable consistently rises as the other rises, whatever the shape of the rise.

| | Pearson $r$ | Spearman $\rho$ |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| Works on | The values | The ranks of the values |
| Measures | Straight-line association | Consistently increasing or decreasing association |
| Affected by outliers | Severely | Barely |
| Needs interval or ratio data | Yes | No, ordinal is enough |
| Reports strength as | How near a straight line | How consistent the ordering is |

## Where they agree

For the 342 complete penguin records:

| Pair | Pearson | Spearman |
| -------------------------- | ------: | -------: |
| Flipper length and body mass | +0.871 | +0.840 |
| Bill length and bill depth | −0.235 | −0.222 |

Both pairs give nearly the same answer either way. When a relationship is roughly linear and free of extreme values, the two coefficients agree, and the choice between them hardly matters.

That agreement is itself information. Comparing the two is a quick diagnostic, and a large gap tells you something specific is going on.

## Where they diverge: a curved relationship

Take a variable that grows exponentially against a steadily increasing one, with a little noise added. Fifty points, generated with a fixed seed:

```
Pearson  r = 0.810
Spearman ρ = 0.966
```

Spearman is close to 1 because the relationship is perfectly consistent: every increase in one variable comes with an increase in the other. Pearson is lower because the pattern is a curve, and a straight line fits it only moderately well.

Read the pair together and you learn something neither gives alone. A strong Spearman with a weaker Pearson means the relationship is dependable but not straight.

## Where they diverge: one bad value

Take the flipper length and body mass columns, which correlate at +0.871. Change one bird's mass from 3,750 g to 25,000 g, the kind of error a stray digit produces:

| | Clean data | One mistyped value |
| -------- | ---------: | -----------------: |
| Pearson | 0.871 | 0.442 |
| Spearman | 0.840 | 0.827 |

Pearson halves. Spearman moves by 0.013.

The reason is direct. Pearson works with distances, and 25,000 is enormously far from everything else, so that one point dominates the calculation. Spearman only knows that this bird is now the heaviest, which changes its rank from somewhere in the middle to last, and one rank changing among 342 barely registers.

> A large gap between Pearson and Spearman is a signal, not a nuisance. It means either a curve or an extreme value, and both are worth finding before you report anything.

## Choosing between them

| Situation | Use |
| -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Roughly linear, no extreme values | Pearson |
| Relationship is monotonic but curved | Spearman |
| Outliers present and you are unsure if they are errors | Spearman, and investigate them |
| One or both variables are ordinal, such as ratings | Spearman |
| You need $r^2$ to talk about variation explained | Pearson |
| First look at an unfamiliar dataset | Both, and compare |

The last row is the practical recommendation. Computing both costs nothing, and their disagreement is diagnostic.

## What Spearman does not fix

Spearman is resistant to outliers and indifferent to curvature, and it is not a general solution.

It cannot see non-monotonic relationships. A pattern that rises then falls has no consistent ordering, so Spearman lands near zero just as Pearson does.

It discards magnitude entirely. Two variables where a huge increase in one accompanies a tiny increase in the other will show a Spearman of 1, because ranks record order and not size.

And it does nothing whatsoever about the grouping problem, where a relationship inside groups differs from the relationship across them. Both coefficients fall for it equally, which is the subject of the next topic.

## Practise the comparison

Take any two numeric columns and compute both coefficients.

Then create a curve on purpose: square one of the columns and correlate it against the original. Pearson will drop below 1 while Spearman stays at exactly 1, because squaring preserves order perfectly while destroying straightness.

Then introduce one typo, multiplying a single value by ten, and recompute both. Seeing which coefficient survives which attack fixes the difference between them better than a definition does.
