## Putting a relationship on a fixed scale

Covariance gets the direction of a relationship right and leaves you unable to say how strong it is. Its value changes if you switch from millimetres to centimetres, and two covariances measured on different pairs of columns cannot be compared.

The correlation coefficient fixes this with one adjustment: divide the covariance by both standard deviations. The units cancel, and what remains always falls between −1 and +1.

## The formula

$$r = \frac{\text{cov}(x, y)}{s_x \, s_y}$$

Here $\text{cov}(x,y)$ is the covariance of the two variables, and $s_x$ and $s_y$ are their sample standard deviations. Written out in full:

$$r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \bar{y})^2}}$$

Every symbol is one you have met: $x_i$ and $y_i$ are the paired measurements, $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$ their means, $n$ the number of pairs.

This quantity is called Pearson's correlation coefficient, and $r$ is the standard symbol for it.

## What the number means

| $r$ | What it says |
| --- | ------------------------------------------- |
| +1 | Every point sits exactly on an upward straight line |
| +0.7 | Strong upward tendency with visible scatter |
| +0.3 | Weak upward tendency |
| 0 | No straight-line tendency at all |
| −0.5 | Moderate downward tendency |
| −1 | Every point sits exactly on a downward straight line |

The two covariances from before become comparable once converted:

| Pair | Covariance | $r$ |
| -------------------------- | ---------: | -----: |
| Flipper length and body mass | 9,824.4 | +0.871 |
| Bill length and bill depth | −2.534 | −0.235 |

Now the comparison is possible. Flipper length and mass are strongly and positively related. Bill length and depth are weakly and negatively related in this file. The covariances gave no hint of that difference in strength.

And unlike covariance, $r$ does not move when units change. Bill length in centimetres against depth in millimetres still gives −0.235.

> Correlation is covariance with the units divided out. Everything it measures, covariance measured first; correlation only makes it readable.

## Interpreting strength honestly

Labels like "strong" and "weak" depend entirely on the field. An $r$ of 0.6 between two physical measurements of the same animal is unremarkable. The same value between a teaching intervention and exam performance would be extraordinary.

One transformation helps. Squaring $r$ gives the proportion of variation in one variable that is accounted for by the straight-line relationship with the other.

| $r$ | $r^2$ | Variation accounted for |
| ---: | ----: | ----------------------: |
| 0.9 | 0.81 | 81% |
| 0.7 | 0.49 | 49% |
| 0.5 | 0.25 | 25% |
| 0.3 | 0.09 | 9% |

That table punctures a common overstatement. An $r$ of 0.5 sounds like half a relationship and accounts for a quarter of the variation.

## The four things correlation cannot see

This is where most misuse begins, and each failure is worth recognising on sight.

Curved relationships defeat it. A pattern that rises then falls can produce an $r$ near zero while being perfectly predictable. Correlation measures straight-line tendency only.

Outliers distort it badly. In the penguin file, flipper length and mass correlate at +0.871. Change a single bird's mass from 3,750 g to 25,000 g, the kind of error a stray digit produces, and $r$ collapses to +0.442. One row in 342 halved the measured strength of a real relationship.

Groups can reverse it. The negative correlation between bill length and depth across all birds becomes positive inside every species. That reversal has its own topic and is the most important caution in this unit.

And correlation says nothing about which variable drives the other, or whether either does.

## Sample size and significance

A correlation computed from few points is unstable. With five observations, an $r$ of 0.6 is entirely ordinary under pure chance; with three hundred, it would be remarkable.

Software will report a p-value alongside $r$, testing whether the true correlation is zero. With large samples, tiny correlations become significant, which returns to the theme from the previous unit: significance answers whether chance explains the result, and $r$ itself answers how strong it is. Report both.

## Practise on two pairs

Take a dataset with at least three numeric columns and compute $r$ for two different pairs.

For each, write the value, its square as a percentage, and one sentence in plain language. Then deliberately corrupt one value in one column, changing a number by a factor of ten as a typo would, and recompute.

Watching a strong correlation collapse because of a single digit teaches more about the fragility of this statistic than any warning, and it is exactly what happens in real files where nobody checked the extremes first.
