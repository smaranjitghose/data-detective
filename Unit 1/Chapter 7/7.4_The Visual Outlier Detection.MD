## Seeing what a threshold cannot

The interquartile and z-score methods return a list of flagged rows. They answer whether a value crosses a line, and they answer nothing else.

Looking at the shape of the data answers different questions: whether the flagged points sit just past the line or far beyond it, whether the rest of the data is one cluster or two, and whether a value is odd only in combination with another column. None of those show up in a count.

## What a box plot draws

A box plot is the interquartile method rendered as a picture, so its parts map exactly onto the fences from earlier.

| Part of the plot | What it shows |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| The box | From $Q_1$ to $Q_3$, holding the middle half |
| The line inside it | The median |
| The whiskers | Out to the furthest value still inside the fences |
| Points beyond | Values outside the fences, drawn individually |

Reading one takes seconds. A box sitting low with a long upper whisker means a right-skewed distribution. A median line sitting off centre inside the box means the middle half itself is lopsided. Isolated points beyond a whisker are the flagged values, and their distance from the whisker tells you how extreme they are.

For penguin body mass across all 342 birds, the box would run from 3,550 to 4,750 grams with the median at 4,050, no points drawn beyond the whiskers, and a slight stretch upward. For Chinstraps alone, two points would appear outside, at 4,800 and 2,700 grams.

## Comparing groups is where box plots earn their place

Drawing one box per species, side by side, shows in a single view what several tables of numbers said across the last two topics: Gentoos sit far above the other two, Adelies and Chinstraps overlap almost completely, and the two Chinstrap outliers are outliers only relative to their own species.

That is the practical use. One box for one column tells you little that quartiles do not. Several boxes side by side compare distributions, spreads, and outlier patterns at once.

## Scatter plots find what single columns cannot

Some values are unremarkable in every column and strange in combination.

Consider a bird whose bill length is typical of Gentoos and whose bill depth is typical of Adelies. Both values sit comfortably inside their columns' ranges, so no single-column method flags anything. Plotting the two columns against each other places that bird alone in an empty region between the species clusters.

| What you are looking for | What it looks like |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| A single odd combination | One point isolated from every cluster |
| A separate group hiding in the file | A second cluster, well away from the main one |
| A data entry error | A point far along one axis, ordinary on the other |
| A recording rule you did not know | A hard straight edge, or a line of points at one value |

That last row catches things no threshold method can. A column that never exceeds 100 because the form capped it, or a stripe of points at exactly zero because zero means missing, appears immediately as a shape and never as a flagged value.

> A detection rule tells you which points crossed a line. Looking at the data tells you why the line is where it is, and whether it belongs there.

## What to look for, in order

Four questions cover most of what a first look should answer.

Is there one cluster or several? Several usually means a grouping column you have not used yet.

Are the extreme points continuous with the rest, or separated by a gap? A value trailing off gradually from the bulk is probably part of the distribution. One sitting alone with empty space between it and everything else is more likely an error.

Does the spread change across the range? Values fanning out as they get larger is common with quantities like income and spending, and it means a single spread measure describes the data poorly.

Are there impossible shapes? Straight vertical or horizontal lines of points, values at exactly zero, and hard boundaries all indicate a recording rule rather than a measurement.

## The limits of looking

Visual inspection scales badly. It works for a handful of columns and becomes impractical when there are fifty, and it cannot be automated into a pipeline that runs every night.

It is also subjective. Two people looking at the same picture will disagree about whether a point is separated enough to matter, which is exactly the ambiguity that numeric thresholds exist to remove.

The two approaches are complements. Use a rule to produce a candidate list, then look at the data to decide what the candidates mean, then say which rule you applied so someone else can reproduce your list.

## Practise reading before computing

Take a numeric column and a grouping column from any dataset.

Before computing anything, write down where you expect the middle half to sit and whether you expect outliers. Then compute the quartiles and fences and compare against your guess.

Then do the same exercise for two numeric columns together, predicting whether they form one cloud or several. Being wrong is the useful outcome, because the gap between what you expected and what the data holds is precisely what exploration is for.
