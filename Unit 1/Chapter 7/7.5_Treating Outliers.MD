## Four responses, and the wrong one is the default

You have found the unusual values. The Chinstrap penguin at 2,700 grams, the mistyped 25,000 gram bird, the customer whose order is fifty times the typical size.

The reflex is to delete them. That reflex is wrong often enough to be worth replacing with a decision, and the decision has four options.

## The options

| Option | What you do | When it fits |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Remove | Drop the row from the analysis | The value is demonstrably an error and cannot be corrected |
| Correct | Fix the value from a reliable source | You can establish what it should have been |
| Transform | Change the scale, usually with logarithms | The whole distribution is skewed, not just a few points |
| Cap | Replace extremes with a boundary value | Extremes are real but distort a specific calculation |
| Keep | Change nothing | The value is real, or it is what you are studying |

Removal sits at the top because it is what people do, not because it is what they should do first. Correction is better whenever it is possible, because it preserves the row.

## Removing, and what it costs

Deleting a row deletes every other measurement on that row too. A bird with a mistyped mass still has a valid bill length, flipper length, and species, and dropping the row discards all of it.

The alternative is to blank out the single bad value and keep the rest of the row, which means the row is complete for some analyses and incomplete for others. That is usually the better trade, and it is the standard practice.

Removal is defensible when the value is impossible, when you cannot recover the correct figure, and when the number of affected rows is small enough that losing them does not distort the sample. Removing 2% of rows is a footnote. Removing 20% is a different dataset.

## Transforming, and what it changes

For distributions with a long right tail, the extreme values are not errors and are not rare accidents. They are the shape.

Taking logarithms compresses the upper end and pulls such a distribution towards symmetry. Income, city populations, file sizes, and waiting times all respond well to it.

The cost is that your results are now about the transformed scale. A difference in mean log income is not a difference in mean income, and converting back requires care. Transformation is a legitimate tool that changes the question slightly, and reporting on the original scale wherever possible keeps readers oriented.

## Capping, and the honesty problem

Capping, sometimes called winsorising, replaces every value above a chosen percentile with that percentile's value. Set the cap at the 99th percentile and the top 1% all become equal to it.

This keeps the rows, limits the influence of extremes, and does something dishonest in the process: it invents values. A customer who spent ten lakh is now recorded as having spent whatever the 99th percentile was.

It has a place in specific calculations where a single value would otherwise dominate, and it has no place in reporting. Never cap and then describe the maximum.

## Keeping, which is more often right than students expect

Three situations call for changing nothing.

The value is real and the analysis should reflect reality. A file of Indian city populations that excludes Mumbai and Delhi because they are outliers describes a country that does not exist.

The outliers are the subject. Fraud, equipment failure, disease clusters, security intrusions: in each of these the rare cases are what the analysis is for.

Or the method you are using does not care. Medians, interquartile ranges, and rank-based correlations are barely affected by extremes, so a switch of method sometimes removes the problem more honestly than a change of data.

> Choosing a resistant method is often better than changing the data. The data was a record of what happened; the method is only a choice about how to describe it.

## The rule that makes any choice defensible

Whatever you decide, run the analysis both ways and see whether the conclusion changes.

If it does not, the decision was not important and you can say so in one line. If it does, the outliers are driving your result, and that fact belongs in the report far more than any of the four treatments does.

Then write down what you did, how many rows it affected, and why. A results section that mentions the treatment of extreme values is more trustworthy than one that does not, because the reader can tell the analyst noticed.

## What never to do

Removing points until a relationship reaches significance is the failure this whole topic exists to prevent. It has a recognisable signature: the removals are all on one side, and the results improve at every step.

Silently deleting is nearly as bad. If a reader cannot tell from your report that anything was removed, they cannot judge whether it should have been.

## Practise both ways

Take a dataset with at least one extreme value and a relationship you can compute, such as a correlation between two columns.

Compute it with the extremes and again without. Note the difference, then write the two sentences you would use to report each version.

Then decide which you would publish and write one line justifying it. Doing that in a low-stakes exercise builds the habit you will need when the decision changes a conclusion someone is paying for.
