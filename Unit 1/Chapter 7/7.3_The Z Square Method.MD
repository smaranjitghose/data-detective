## Counting standard deviations from the mean

A second approach to flagging unusual values asks a direct question: how many standard deviations from the mean does this value sit?

$$z = \frac{x - \bar{x}}{s}$$

Here $x$ is the value, $\bar{x}$ the sample mean, and $s$ the sample standard deviation. A $z$ of 2 means the value sits two standard deviations above the mean, and −1.5 means one and a half below.

The common rule flags anything with $|z| > 3$.

## Where the threshold comes from

The empirical rule supplies it. In roughly normal data, about 99.7% of values fall within three standard deviations of the mean, so beyond that lies roughly 3 in every 1,000 observations.

| Threshold | Expected share flagged in normal data |
| --------- | ------------------------------------: |
| $|z| > 2$ | About 5% |
| $|z| > 2.5$ | About 1.2% |
| $|z| > 3$ | About 0.3% |
| $|z| > 3.5$ | About 0.05% |

That reliance is the method's defining feature. The whole calibration comes from the normal distribution, and applying it to data that is not roughly normal makes the percentages meaningless.

## On the penguin masses

The 342 recorded body masses have a mean of 4,201.8 grams and a standard deviation of 802.0 grams.

The heaviest bird, at 6,300 grams, gives a $z$ of 2.62. The lightest, at 2,700 grams, gives −1.87. Nothing in the file exceeds 3, and nine birds exceed 2.

That agrees with what the interquartile method found, which is reassuring. Two methods built on different principles reaching the same verdict is worth more than either alone.

## The problem the method has with itself

The mean and standard deviation are both computed from the data, including the outliers you are trying to find. Extreme values therefore corrupt the very yardstick used to measure them.

Add one mistyped 25,000 gram bird to the penguin masses and watch what happens:

| | Original | With one typo |
| ------------------ | -------: | ------------: |
| Mean | 4,201.8 | 4,263.9 |
| Standard deviation | 802.0 | 1,381.0 |

The standard deviation inflated by 72%. Every other bird's $z$ score shrank correspondingly, because the yardstick got longer.

With one such value the culprit is still caught, at $z = 15.02$. With three or four together, they inflate the standard deviation enough to hide each other, and the method reports nothing wrong. This is called masking, and it is the reason the interquartile method is usually the safer first pass.

> A method that uses the mean and standard deviation to find extreme values is using statistics that extreme values distort. The interquartile method avoids this by using positions instead.

## Groups break it too

The mean and standard deviation of a mixed population describe no group in it.

An Adelie penguin weighing 3,700 grams is entirely typical of its species. Measured against the pooled mean it has a $z$ of −0.63, unremarkable. Measured against Gentoos alone, whose mean is 5,076 grams, the same bird gives −2.73, close to being flagged.

Which is correct depends on the question. Neither is correct if you have not decided which population the bird belongs to, and computing z scores across a mixed file amounts to not deciding.

## A more resistant version

Replacing the mean with the median and the standard deviation with a spread measure built on medians gives a version that does not suffer masking. It is usually called the modified z-score, and it flags extreme values reliably even when several are present at once.

| | Standard z-score | Modified z-score | IQR fences |
| ----------------- | ---------------- | ---------------- | ---------- |
| Built on | Mean and standard deviation | Median-based spread | Quartiles |
| Resistant to masking | No | Yes | Yes |
| Needs roughly normal data | Yes | Less so | Less so |
| Familiar to most readers | Yes | No | Yes |

## When the z-score method is the right choice

Three situations suit it.

The data is roughly normal, ideally checked rather than assumed. It is a single population, not a mixture. And you want a value's unusualness expressed on a scale that transfers across columns, which is the one thing the interquartile method cannot do.

That last point is a real advantage. A $z$ of 2.6 means the same degree of unusualness whether the column holds grams or millimetres, so z scores make different measurements comparable in a way that raw distances never can.

## Practise the masking

Take a numeric column and compute its mean, standard deviation, and the z score of its largest value.

Then add three fabricated extreme values, each around ten times the column's maximum, and recompute everything.

Watch the standard deviation inflate and the z scores of your three additions come out lower than the single value's score did before. Producing masking deliberately, once, is what makes the warning stick.
