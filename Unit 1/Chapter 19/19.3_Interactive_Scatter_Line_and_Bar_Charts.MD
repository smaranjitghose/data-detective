## The three basic charts, with interaction added

The chart types are the ones from the Matplotlib unit. What changes is what a reader can do with them afterwards.

## Scatter, and hovering to identify

```python
import plotly.express as px
import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv('penguins.csv')

fig = px.scatter(df, x='flipper_length_mm', y='body_mass_g',
                 color='species', hover_data=['island', 'sex'],
                 opacity=0.7)
```

The two clusters appear as before. Hovering over any point reports its measurements, island, and sex.

For a chart with many points, `opacity` handles overplotting the same way `alpha` does in Matplotlib. Zooming handles the rest: dragging a rectangle over a dense region redraws it at that scale, which no static chart can offer.

Double-clicking resets the view, which is worth telling readers who have not used an interactive chart before.

## Line charts, and the range slider

```python
a = pd.read_csv('aapl.csv', parse_dates=['Date'])

fig = px.line(a, x='Date', y='AAPL.Close',
              title='Apple closing price, Feb 2015 to Feb 2017')
fig.update_xaxes(rangeslider_visible=True)
```

The 506 daily closes from the earlier unit, now with a slider beneath the chart for selecting a period.

This is the case where interaction earns its place most clearly. Two years of daily data at full width compresses each month into a few pixels; the slider lets a reader look at any month without a second chart being made.

Plotly sorts by the x axis and handles datetime axes automatically, as seaborn does and plain Matplotlib does not.

## Bar charts, and the same baseline rule

```python
means = df.groupby('species', as_index=False)['body_mass_g'].mean()

fig = px.bar(means, x='species', y='body_mass_g')
fig.update_yaxes(range=[0, 6000])
```

Interaction changes nothing about the encoding rules. A bar still represents a length from a baseline, so the axis still has to start at zero, and Plotly will choose otherwise if you let it.

The arithmetic from the visualisation principles unit applies unchanged: a true height ratio of 1.37 becomes an apparent 14.61 when the axis starts at 3,600.

Setting the range explicitly is the same one-line habit as in Matplotlib.

## Grouped and stacked bars

```python
grouped = df.groupby(['species', 'sex'], as_index=False)['body_mass_g'].mean()
px.bar(grouped, x='species', y='body_mass_g', color='sex', barmode='group')
```

`barmode='group'` places the bars side by side; `'stack'` piles them. The default is stacking, which is worth knowing since grouped is usually what you want for comparison.

The reasoning from the encoding unit stands: only the bottom segment of a stack has a common baseline, so stacking beyond two categories obstructs the comparison.

## Legend toggling

Every chart with a `color` argument gets a clickable legend. Clicking an entry hides that group; double-clicking isolates it.

This is the most useful interaction in practice and it requires nothing from you. A reader looking at three species can view any pair, or any one alone, without asking for another chart.

It has a consequence for how you build charts: a chart with five groups is more defensible in Plotly than in Matplotlib, because the reader can reduce the clutter themselves.

## Animation over time

```python
g = px.data.gapminder()
px.scatter(g, x='gdpPercap', y='lifeExp', size='pop', color='continent',
           hover_name='country', animation_frame='year',
           log_x=True, size_max=55, range_y=[25, 90])
```

`animation_frame` adds a play control that steps through the values of a column. The gapminder data holds 142 countries across twelve years from 1952 to 2007, and the animation shows the cloud moving up and right over that period.

Note `log_x=True`. Income per head spans several orders of magnitude, and the axis-control reasoning from the Matplotlib unit applies: linear axes make the low end unreadable.

> Animation shows change and hides comparison. A reader cannot compare 1962 against 1987 while the chart is moving, and a small-multiples grid answers that question better.

## What stays the same

Interaction does not repair a badly chosen chart. The chart selection rules, the encoding ranking, the zero baseline, and the caution about dual axes all apply exactly as before.

What interaction changes is the follow-up question, not the first impression.

## Practise the three

Rebuild one scatter plot, one line chart, and one bar chart from earlier units in Plotly.

Add `hover_data` to the scatter, a range slider to the line, and an explicit zero-based y range to the bars.

Then hand all three to someone and watch what they click. Most people find legend toggling on their own and never discover the range slider, which tells you something about what to rely on when a chart has to communicate.
