## Values shaded onto geography

When each row of your data belongs to a place, a map is the chart. A choropleth colours each region by a value: life expectancy per country, rainfall per district, sales per state.

```python
import plotly.express as px

g = px.data.gapminder()
latest = g[g.year == 2007]

fig = px.choropleth(latest, locations='iso_alpha', color='lifeExp',
                    hover_name='country',
                    color_continuous_scale='viridis')
```

The gapminder dataset holds 1,704 rows covering 142 countries from 1952 to 2007. The 2007 slice has one row per country, with life expectancy running from 39.6 to 82.6 years.

The map shades each country by that value, with hovering giving the country name and figure. India in 2007 shows a life expectancy of 64.7 years, a population of 1.11 billion, and GDP per head of 2,452 dollars.

## Matching your data to the map

The hardest part of a choropleth is not drawing it. It is getting your region names to match the ones the map knows.

| `locationmode` | Expects | Example |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------- | -------------- |
| `ISO-3` | Three-letter country codes | `IND`, `USA` |
| `country names` | Full country names | `India` |
| `USA-states` | Two-letter state codes | `TN`, `CA` |

The gapminder data includes an `iso_alpha` column of ISO-3 codes, which is why the example works with no configuration.

Country names are far more fragile. Is it `India` or `Republic of India`, `South Korea` or `Korea, Rep.`? A country whose name does not match is silently omitted from the map, which is the same failure as an unmatched join key and just as invisible.

Always compare the number of regions in your data against the number drawn. A country missing from a map looks like a country with no data.

## Regions the built-in maps do not cover

Plotly ships with country and US state boundaries. For Indian states and districts, or any other administrative geography, you supply the boundaries yourself as GeoJSON.

```python
fig = px.choropleth(df, geojson=india_states, locations='state',
                    featureidkey='properties.ST_NM', color='literacy_rate')
```

`featureidkey` names the field inside the GeoJSON that holds the region identifier, and your `locations` column must match it exactly.

The matching problem multiplies here. Indian state names appear in several spellings across datasets, and the boundaries themselves change when districts are reorganised, so the vintage of the GeoJSON has to match the vintage of the data.

## The colour scale is a decision

The palette rules from the seaborn unit apply unchanged.

A sequential scale such as `viridis` for a quantity with a natural low and high. A diverging scale for values around a meaningful midpoint, such as change since last year. Never a qualitative palette for a continuous value.

```python
fig = px.choropleth(latest, locations='iso_alpha', color='lifeExp',
                    range_color=[35, 85])
```

Fixing `range_color` matters when you draw several maps that will be compared. Automatic scaling means each map uses its own range, so the same colour represents different values in two maps sitting side by side.

## The problem with area

A choropleth encodes value as colour and area as geography, and readers cannot separate the two.

Large regions dominate visually regardless of what they contain. A map of a country shaded by district makes the sparsely populated large districts loud and the dense small ones nearly invisible, even when the small ones hold most of the people.

This is a real distortion rather than a nuisance. The same data drawn as a sorted bar chart of the top twenty regions often communicates better, and it is worth drawing both before choosing.

> A choropleth shows where, and it weights that showing by physical size. If the story is about people rather than land, the map is systematically misleading and looks authoritative.

## Rates, not counts

Colouring a map by a raw count usually produces a map of population.

Cases, sales, complaints, and incidents all scale with how many people live somewhere, so the map shows population with extra steps. Dividing by population, area, or another denominator turns it into a rate, which is what the reader thinks they are seeing anyway.

## Practise the match

Take any dataset with a region column and draw a choropleth.

Before looking at the map, count the distinct regions in your data. Then count how many appear shaded.

Any gap is unmatched names, and finding them takes the same set-difference check as an unmatched join. That check, done first, prevents publishing a map with three states quietly absent.
