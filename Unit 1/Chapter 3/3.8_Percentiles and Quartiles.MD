## Where a value sits, rather than what it is

A JEE result arrives with two numbers: marks, and a percentile. A student scores 156 marks and lands at the 98.7th percentile.

The marks say what they achieved. The percentile says where that achievement sits among everyone who took the exam, and for admission purposes only the second one matters. A raw score means nothing without knowing whether the paper was hard that year and how everyone else did.

That is what percentiles are for. They convert a value into a position.

## The definition, and the thing everyone gets wrong

A percentile is a value below which a given proportion of the data falls. The 25th percentile is the value with a quarter of the data at or below it.

The confusion worth killing immediately: 98.7th percentile does not mean 98.7% of the marks. It means roughly 98.7% of candidates scored at or below that student. A student can sit at the 98.7th percentile with 156 marks out of 300, and both numbers are correct because they measure different things.

| | What it measures | Depends on |
| ------------ | ------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Percentage | How much of the total you got | Only your own performance |
| Percentile | How many people you are above | Everyone else's performance too |

Quantiles are the same idea with a different divisor. Quartiles cut the data into four parts, deciles into ten, percentiles into a hundred. The word quantile covers all of them.

## Finding them on real numbers

The nine recorded penguin masses, sorted:

```
3250, 3450, 3475, 3625, 3650, 3750, 3800, 4250, 4675
```

The median is the middle value, 3,650 grams, and it is also the 50th percentile and the second quartile.

The first quartile sits one quarter of the way through the sorted values, at 3,475 grams. The third quartile sits three quarters of the way through, at 3,800 grams.

| Name | Also called | Value here | Means |
| ---- | -------------- | ---------: | ------------------------------ |
| Q1 | 25th percentile | 3,475 g | A quarter of the birds are lighter |
| Q2 | 50th percentile, median | 3,650 g | Half are lighter |
| Q3 | 75th percentile | 3,800 g | Three quarters are lighter |

Those three numbers plus the smallest and largest values give the five-number summary: 3,250, 3,475, 3,650, 3,800, 4,675. Five numbers describe the whole distribution well enough to spot lopsidedness. The gap from Q3 to the maximum is 875 grams while the gap from the minimum to Q1 is 225, which says the heavy end is stretched.

## Why your software may disagree with you

Ask two tools for the 25th percentile of the same nine numbers and you can get different answers.

With nine values, the quarter-way point does not land exactly on an observation, so something has to decide what to do. Some methods take the nearest value, others interpolate between the two neighbours, and there are several accepted conventions.

The differences are small and they are real, and they matter when a cutoff is being applied to people. If a scholarship goes to everyone above the 75th percentile, the method decides who is on which side of the line for candidates sitting near it.

> Percentiles are defined by position, and positions between two data points have to be resolved by a rule. Know which rule your tool uses before a decision depends on it.

## What percentiles are good for

Three jobs come up constantly.

Comparing across different scales is the first. A student at the 90th percentile in physics and the 60th in chemistry has a clear profile, even though the two papers had different difficulty and different marking. Raw marks would not support that comparison.

Setting cutoffs is the second. Any rule of the form "the top 5% qualify" is a percentile rule, and it adapts automatically to a hard year or an easy one.

Describing shape is the third, and it is what exploratory work uses most. The five-number summary, and the interquartile range that comes from it, describe a distribution without assuming anything about its shape.

## Where percentile thinking goes wrong

Percentiles hide the size of the gaps between people. In a tightly bunched exam, the 60th and 90th percentiles might be four marks apart, and the ranking implies a difference in ability that four marks cannot support.

They also depend entirely on who is in the comparison group. A student at the 95th percentile within their college and the 60th nationally has not changed; the reference group has. Any reported percentile without a stated group is close to meaningless.

And in small datasets they are unstable. With nine penguins, one more bird moves the quartiles noticeably. Percentiles are a large-sample tool, and reporting the 90th percentile of twelve values suggests a precision that is not there.

## Practise where it affects you

Find a percentile that has been applied to you: an exam result, a fitness app's ranking, a growth chart from childhood.

Write down three things. The comparison group it was computed against. What the raw value was. And how much the raw value would have to change to move you ten percentile points.

That third question is the one that reveals whether the ranking is describing a real difference or a crowded middle, and it is the question almost nobody asks about their own results.
