## Describing what you have, and claiming what you do not

Your class sits an exam. You collect all sixty marks, add them up, divide, and announce the class average.

Nobody can argue with that number. You had every mark, you did the arithmetic, and the average is a fact about those sixty students. There is no uncertainty in it at all.

Now a newspaper reports that engineering students across the state find the first-year programming paper hardest. They did not ask every student in the state. They asked a few thousand, and stretched the answer to cover several hundred thousand people they never contacted.

That second move is a different kind of work. It can be done well or badly, it is never certain, and it is where most of the interesting mistakes in statistics live.

## The two jobs, side by side

| | Descriptive | Inferential |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| What it covers | Only the rows you actually have | A larger group you did not fully measure |
| What it produces | A summary: counts, averages, spread | An estimate, with a statement of how uncertain it is |
| Can it be wrong? | Only if you miscalculate | Yes, even when the arithmetic is perfect |
| What it needs | The data | The data, plus knowing how it was collected |
| Typical sentence | "The average mark in this class was 62" | "Marks across the state are probably near 62" |

The row that matters most is the third. Descriptive work fails only through error. Inferential work can be done correctly and still deliver the wrong answer, because the group you measured was not like the group you are talking about.

## Where the penguin file sits

The Palmer Station file records 344 penguins across three species. Count them and you get 152 Adelie, 68 Chinstrap, and 124 Gentoo. That is description: a complete, exact statement about the birds in the file.

Say instead that Gentoo penguins have longer flippers than Adelie penguins, meaning penguins in general and not only these 344, and you have crossed into inference. The claim now depends on whether the birds that got caught and measured resemble the birds that did not.

The distinction is not about the arithmetic. Both statements might involve the same calculation. It is about how far the sentence reaches beyond the rows.

> Descriptive statistics answers "what is in this data". Inferential statistics answers "what is probably true of the world this data came from".

## Why the second one is hard

Two different things can go wrong when you stretch a result beyond your rows, and they are worth separating early because they have different fixes.

The first is ordinary variation. Ask fifty students instead of all sixty and you get a slightly different average, purely because you happened to include different people. Ask another fifty and you get another number. This scatter is unavoidable, it shrinks as the sample grows, and statistics has well-developed machinery for quantifying it.

The second is a systematic mismatch between who you measured and who you are talking about. A survey circulated through a college WhatsApp group reaches students who are in that group, active on their phones, and inclined to answer. If those students differ from the rest in any way connected to your question, your estimate is wrong in a fixed direction, and no amount of extra responses will fix it.

More data cures the first problem. Nothing except better collection cures the second.

## Which kind of claim is this?

| The statement | Which kind | Why |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| 44 of the Adelie penguins in the file were on Biscoe | Descriptive | A count of rows you have |
| The average mark in this class was 62 | Descriptive | Every student was measured |
| Gentoo penguins have longer flippers than Adelies | Inferential | Reaches beyond the 344 birds |
| 30% of Indian households own a car | Inferential | Nobody surveyed every household |
| This dataset has 11 rows with no sex recorded | Descriptive | A property of the file itself |

Notice the last row. Statements about a dataset's own flaws are always descriptive, and they are among the most useful things you will report.

## Why this course leans on description

Exploratory work is mostly descriptive, and deliberately so.

Before anyone can responsibly estimate anything about a wider population, someone has to establish what is actually in the file: how many rows, what the values look like, where the gaps are, which groups are represented and which are thin. That work is exact, checkable, and it decides whether inference is even worth attempting.

A file with 68 Chinstrap penguins can support description of those 68 perfectly well. Whether it can support a claim about Chinstrap penguins as a species depends entirely on how those 68 were selected, which is a question about collection rather than calculation.

## Say which one you are doing

When you write a sentence about data, check where it stops.

If it stops at the rows in front of you, it is descriptive, and your only obligation is to compute it correctly. If it reaches past them, it is inferential, and you owe the reader two extra things: how the data was collected, and some sense of how much the answer might move if a different sample had been drawn.

Practise on something small. Take any dataset you can download and write four sentences about it: two that stop at the file, and two that reach beyond it. For each of the second pair, write one more line naming the group you are claiming to describe and one reason your data might not represent it well.

That last line is the whole discipline. Most bad statistics is a descriptive sentence wearing an inferential coat.
