## Not every number is a quantity

Open any admission form. It asks for your age, your roll number, your pin code, your course, and how strongly you agree that the hostel food is acceptable.

Four of those five answers can be typed as digits. Only one of them is a quantity in any useful sense.

Add two ages and you get a meaningful total. Add two pin codes and you get nonsense. Roll numbers can be sorted but not averaged. The digits look identical in a spreadsheet, and a program will happily average all of them, which is exactly the problem.

## The first split: measured or labelled

Every column falls into one of two families.

Quantitative variables record an amount. Body mass in grams, marks out of a hundred, number of assignments submitted. Arithmetic on them means something.

Qualitative variables, also called categorical, record a category. Species, city, course, whether a student is a hosteller. You can count how often each category occurs, but you cannot add or average the categories themselves.

The test is not whether the value looks like a number. It is whether adding two values produces something meaningful.

| Column | Family | Adding two values gives |
| ---------------- | ------------ | ------------------------------------ |
| Body mass (g) | Quantitative | A meaningful combined mass |
| Marks obtained | Quantitative | A meaningful total |
| Pin code | Qualitative | Nothing |
| Roll number | Qualitative | Nothing |
| Species | Qualitative | Nothing |
| Number of siblings | Quantitative | A meaningful count |

## Inside quantitative: counted or measured

Quantitative columns divide again, and the difference decides which summaries and which charts suit them.

Discrete variables come from counting. Number of siblings, assignments submitted, penguins on an island. The values are whole and there is nothing between two of them. A student cannot submit 4.5 assignments.

Continuous variables come from measuring. Body mass, flipper length, time taken, temperature. Between any two values there is another one, and the only limit is your instrument. A penguin recorded at 3750 grams is not exactly 3750 grams; it is 3750 to the nearest whatever the scale reads.

The Palmer file has both. Flipper length in millimetres is continuous, recorded as whole numbers because the ruler stopped there. Counting the birds per island produces discrete values: 44, 56, 52.

> Discrete answers "how many". Continuous answers "how much". The gap matters when you choose a chart and when you decide whether an average makes sense.

An average of a discrete variable can land somewhere impossible, and that is fine. A family with 2.3 children does not exist, and the number is still a useful summary of many families.

## Inside qualitative: does the order mean anything

Categorical columns also split, and this one causes real damage when missed.

Some categories have no natural order. Species, city, department. Adelie is not more or less than Gentoo; they are just different labels. You can count them and compare counts, and nothing else.

Others have an order that is real. Satisfaction recorded as poor, fair, good, excellent. Course level recorded as first year through fourth. The order is genuine, so "worse than" and "better than" mean something.

What is missing in the ordered case is a fixed distance. The gap between poor and fair is not guaranteed to equal the gap between good and excellent, because nobody defined those gaps. That is the reason averaging a satisfaction rating is a shakier move than it looks, and it is treated properly in the next topic.

## What each family lets you do

| Family | Sensible summaries | Sensible comparisons |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Qualitative, unordered | Counts, proportions, most common category | Which categories are common or absent |
| Qualitative, ordered | Counts, proportions, middle category | Whether one group sits higher than another |
| Quantitative, discrete | Counts, totals, average, spread | Differences in typical counts |
| Quantitative, continuous | Average, spread, position measures | Differences in typical amounts |

Work down that table and the options widen. This is why identifying the family of every column is the first thing to do with a new dataset: it decides what you are permitted to compute before you compute anything.

## The traps that catch beginners

Three mistakes account for most of the trouble, and all three come from trusting the storage type instead of the meaning.

Numbers used as labels are the commonest. Pin codes, roll numbers, phone numbers, and codes like `1 = male, 2 = female` are all stored as numbers and none is a quantity. An average pin code is not wrong arithmetic; it is a meaningless quantity computed correctly.

Categories stored as text that are actually quantities are the reverse case. A column holding "12 kg" and "9 kg" as text is quantitative information in a qualitative wrapper, and it needs converting before anything works.

Binned quantities are the subtle one. An age column recorded as "18-25", "26-35", "36+" was quantitative when collected and is qualitative now. The person who did the binning threw away information, and you cannot get it back.

## Classify a real file

| Penguin column | Family |
| -------------- | ---------------------- |
| species | Qualitative, unordered |
| island | Qualitative, unordered |
| flipper_length_mm | Quantitative, continuous |
| body_mass_g | Quantitative, continuous |
| sex | Qualitative, unordered |
| year | Ambiguous, and worth arguing about |

That last row is not a trick. A year is stored as a number, has a real order, and equal gaps, yet averaging the years in a study rarely means anything useful. Whether you treat it as a quantity or a label depends on the question you are asking, which is a decision you make rather than something the file tells you.

Take any dataset you have, list every column, and write its family beside it. Mark the ones you are unsure about, because those are where the analysis will later go wrong, and the uncertainty is usually a sign that nobody defined the column carefully in the first place.
