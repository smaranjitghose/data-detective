## What the numbers in a column will let you do

A hostel feedback form records satisfaction as 1 for poor, 2 for fair, 3 for good, 4 for excellent. Two hundred students respond. The warden reports an average satisfaction of 2.7 and calls it a modest improvement on last year's 2.5.

The arithmetic is correct. Whether 2.7 means anything depends on a question nobody asked: is the distance from poor to fair the same as the distance from good to excellent?

Nobody defined those distances. Students interpreted the words privately. The numbers 1 to 4 were labels the form designer chose, and they could equally have been 1, 2, 4, 8.

## Four levels, in increasing strength

The levels of measurement classify columns by what operations their values support. Each level permits everything the level below it permits, plus one thing more.

| Level | What it records | New thing it permits | Example |
| -------- | --------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Nominal | Categories with no order | Counting each category | Species, city, department |
| Ordinal | Categories with a real order | Ranking, and a middle value | Satisfaction, course year, medal position |
| Interval | Ordered values with equal gaps | Meaningful differences | Temperature in Celsius, calendar year |
| Ratio | Interval, plus a true zero | Meaningful ratios | Body mass, marks, distance, count |

The word "permits" is doing the work. Nothing stops you computing an average of nominal codes. The level tells you whether the result carries meaning.

## The two lines people cross

Between nominal and ordinal sits the question of order. Between interval and ratio sits the question of zero, and these are the two places analyses go wrong.

Celsius temperature is interval. The gap between 10 and 20 degrees equals the gap between 30 and 40, so differences are meaningful. But zero Celsius is a convention, the freezing point of water, not an absence of temperature. So 40 degrees is not twice as hot as 20 degrees, and saying so is a genuine error rather than loose talk.

Body mass is ratio. Zero grams means no mass at all, so a 4675 gram penguin really is about 1.4 times the mass of a 3250 gram one.

> The test for ratio is simple. Does zero mean "none of it"? If yes, ratios work. If zero is just a point someone picked, they do not.

Marks out of a hundred are usually treated as ratio, and the treatment is slightly generous. Zero marks does mean no correct answers, but whether a student scoring 80 knows twice as much as one scoring 40 is a claim about learning, not about the scale.

## Back to the satisfaction average

Satisfaction on a four-point word scale is ordinal. You can say that more students chose good than fair, and you can report the middle response. What you cannot safely say is that the average moved from 2.5 to 2.7, because that calculation assumes the four steps are equal in size and nobody ever established that.

What survives at ordinal level:

* The count and proportion choosing each option
* Which option was chosen most often
* The middle response when everyone is lined up in order
* Comparisons of the form "more of this year's students chose good or excellent"

That last one is what the warden actually wanted. The share choosing good or excellent rose from x to y is a defensible sentence built entirely from counts, and it says something a reader can act on.

Averaging ordinal scales is extremely common in practice, particularly with longer scales like 1 to 10, and treating it as a rough indicator rather than a measurement is the honest compromise. Reporting it alongside the counts costs nothing and lets a reader see what the average hid.

## Classifying a real file

| Penguin column | Level | Why |
| ----------------- | ------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| species | Nominal | Three labels, no order |
| island | Nominal | Three labels, no order |
| sex | Nominal | Two labels, no order |
| flipper_length_mm | Ratio | Zero length means no flipper |
| body_mass_g | Ratio | Zero grams means no mass |
| year | Interval | Ordered, equal gaps, but year zero is a convention |

The `year` row shows why interval exists as a separate category. The difference between 2007 and 2009 is two years, which is meaningful. The ratio of 2009 to 2007 is not a sentence anyone would write.

## What each level lets you summarise

| Level | Most common value | Middle value | Average | Spread as a range | Ratios |
| -------- | ----------------- | ------------ | ------- | ----------------- | ------ |
| Nominal | Yes | No | No | No | No |
| Ordinal | Yes | Yes | Doubtful | Rank-based only | No |
| Interval | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | No |
| Ratio | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |

Read that table before you compute anything on an unfamiliar column, and most of the mistakes in this area disappear.

## Practise on a form you have filled in

Take any form you have completed recently: an admission form, a feedback survey, a job application.

List every field and assign a level to each. You will find that most fields are nominal, that the interesting ones are ordinal, and that the form probably stores several nominal fields as numbers.

Then find one ordinal field and write two sentences summarising it: one using an average, one using counts and proportions. Show both to someone who did not fill in the form and ask which tells them more. In my experience the second wins almost every time, and it is also the one you can defend.
