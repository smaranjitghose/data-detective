## How you pick decides what you can claim

You need to know how students at your college feel about the library's opening hours. You have an afternoon and a Google Form.

The fastest route is to post the link in your class group and to two friends who will forward it. Sixty responses arrive by evening. The result says students want the library open later.

Every one of those sixty is a student, and the sample is still close to useless for the question. It reaches your year, your course, and people who read that group chat. Whether final-year students or day scholars want different hours is not something those responses can tell you, and you will never see the gap by looking at the responses themselves.

## The four designs worth knowing

Sampling designs differ in one respect: how a member of the population gets chosen. That mechanism decides what your sample can support.

| Design | How members are chosen | Suits | Main risk |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------- |
| Simple random | Every member has an equal chance, drawn at random | You have a complete list and can reach anyone | Needs that complete list |
| Systematic | Take every kth member from an ordered list | Lists, queues, production lines | A repeating pattern in the list order |
| Stratified | Split into groups, sample randomly within each | Groups differ and all must be represented | You must know the groups in advance |
| Cluster | Split into natural groups, pick whole groups at random | Members are spread out and travel is costly | Clusters may differ from each other |

The one missing from that table is the one everyone actually uses. Convenience sampling means taking whoever is easy to reach, and it is what the Google Form did. It has no place in the table because it supports no claim beyond the people who answered.

## Working through the library question

Say the college has 4,000 students across four years and six departments.

Simple random sampling means getting the full roll list, drawing 300 numbers at random, and chasing those specific students until they respond. It is the cleanest design and the most work, because the students you drew are the students you need, and the ones who ignore you cannot be replaced by whoever is nearby.

Systematic sampling means sorting the roll list and taking every 13th student. Easier to administer, and equivalent to random unless the list order hides a pattern. If the roll list is grouped by department and each department has a multiple of 13 students, your sample could quietly over-represent one department.

Stratified sampling means recognising that year and department probably matter here, dividing the 4,000 into those groups, and drawing randomly inside each. This is usually the right answer for a college survey, because it guarantees final-year students appear rather than leaving it to chance.

Cluster sampling means picking eight tutorial sections at random and surveying everyone in them. Cheap, and it works when sections are interchangeable. If sections are organised by department, you have accidentally sampled departments rather than students.

> The question to ask of any design is not "how many did you get" but "who could never have been included".

## Stratified sampling, slightly closer up

Stratified designs come up constantly in practice, so the mechanics are worth stating.

Suppose the four years hold 1,200, 1,100, 900, and 800 students, and you want 300 responses. Proportional allocation gives each year a share matching its size: 90, 82, 68, and 60 responses, which sums to 300.

The alternative is deliberately over-sampling a small group you care about. If you specifically want to compare final-year students with everyone else, taking only 60 of them may be too few for a stable comparison, and you might take 100 instead. That is legitimate, and it means the overall figure needs weighting to correct for the deliberate imbalance.

## Sample size, and what it does not fix

Bigger samples reduce the scatter that comes from chance. They do nothing at all about a flawed selection method.

A convenience sample of 5,000 students from one department answers the library question worse than a stratified sample of 200 across the college, and it answers it with more confidence, which is the dangerous part.

| Problem | Bigger sample helps? |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------- |
| Estimate wobbles when you repeat it | Yes |
| Small groups too thin to compare | Yes |
| Whole categories of student excluded | No |
| Only the strongly opinionated replied | No |
| The list you sampled from is incomplete | No |

## Non-response is a second sampling stage

A design chooses who you approach. The people who reply choose themselves, and that second selection undoes careful work more often than beginners expect.

Draw a beautiful stratified sample of 300 and have 90 reply, and what you hold is a convenience sample of the willing dressed in a good design. The usual repairs are practical rather than statistical: fewer questions, a reminder, asking in person, and reporting the response rate honestly so a reader can judge.

Recording who did not respond is worth doing whenever possible. If your non-respondents are mostly one year or one department, you know the direction of your problem, which is better than knowing you have one.

## Design a sample this week

Pick a question about your own college that you could actually investigate: satisfaction with lab equipment, hours spent commuting, use of the library after 8 p.m.

Write down the population in one sentence. Choose a design and say why. State the groups you would stratify on and what you expect to differ between them. Then name the two groups most likely to be missing from your responses once they arrive.

Then do the cheap version anyway, and compare what you get against what your design predicted. The gap between the sample you planned and the sample you got is the most instructive thing in this topic, and it never appears in a textbook example.
