## The group you measured and the group you meant

A news channel reports that 62% of Indians prefer online shopping to visiting a store. They did not ask 1.4 billion people. They asked a few thousand, and the sentence quietly covers everyone else.

Every claim of that shape has two groups hiding inside it. The group actually measured, and the group the sentence is about. Statistics is largely the business of managing the distance between them.

## Two words worth getting right early

The population is the entire group you want to say something about. It is defined by your question, not by your data. If you ask about first-year engineering students in Telangana, that is your population, whether or not you can reach them.

The sample is the part you actually measured.

A number computed from the whole population is a parameter. The same number computed from a sample is a statistic. You almost never see a parameter; you see a statistic and use it to guess at the parameter.

| | Population | Sample |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| What it is | Everyone your question covers | The part you measured |
| Size symbol | $N$ | $n$ |
| A summary from it | A parameter, fixed and usually unknown | A statistic, known and varying |
| How often you have it | Rarely | Almost always |

The symbols matter later. $N$ is the number of members of the population, $n$ the number in your sample. When a formula changes depending on which you have, it is because the two situations are different.

## Why not just measure everyone

Sometimes you can. A college can compute the exact average marks of its own students, because it holds every mark. That is a census, and it needs no inference at all.

Usually you cannot, for four ordinary reasons: cost, time, reach, and destruction. Testing every match in a box for whether it lights leaves you with no matches.

The penguin file is a sample, and a useful one to think about. Researchers measured 344 birds. The population is something like "adult foraging penguins of these three species in this archipelago", which is a group nobody could ever measure completely. Every statement about penguins in general rests on how those 344 came to be the ones in the file.

## Two ways a sample misleads you

This distinction is the most valuable idea in the topic, because the two problems look similar and have completely different fixes.

Sampling error is the ordinary scatter that comes from measuring some rather than all. Draw fifty students at random, compute the average, put them back, draw fifty more: the second average differs. Nothing went wrong. The number moves because different people were included.

Sampling bias is a systematic mismatch between who gets measured and who you mean. A survey circulated in a college WhatsApp group reaches students who are in that group and inclined to reply. If those students differ from the rest in a way connected to the question, your answer is off in a fixed direction.

| | Sampling error | Sampling bias |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Cause | Chance | The selection method |
| Direction | Scatters both ways | Consistently one way |
| Effect of more data | Shrinks | Does not shrink at all |
| Fix | A larger sample | A different way of choosing the sample |

> A large biased sample is more dangerous than a small one, because the confident tone it produces is unearned.

## Where the bias usually enters

Three moments account for most of it, and knowing them lets you interrogate any dataset you are handed.

Selection is the first: who could have been included at all. An online form excludes people without reliable internet, and a mess survey conducted at 8 p.m. excludes the students who eat elsewhere, who are exactly the people whose absence you might be studying.

Response is the second: who chose to reply. People with strong opinions answer surveys at higher rates than the indifferent, which is why product ratings cluster at the extremes.

Survival is the third: who is still in the data by the time you see it. Analyse only current students and you have excluded everyone who dropped out, which makes any study of why students leave close to impossible with that file.

## Interrogating a sample you did not draw

Four questions, and they are worth asking of every dataset you meet.

| Question | What a bad answer sounds like |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Who could have been included? | "Everyone who saw the link" |
| Who actually was? | "Whoever responded" |
| Who is systematically absent? | "Nobody, we sent it to all students" |
| Would the absent differ on my question? | "Probably not" without a reason |

The fourth question is the one that decides whether the analysis is worth doing. If the people missing from your data are likely to differ on exactly the thing you are measuring, the sample cannot answer your question no matter how large it is.

## Practise on a survey you have seen

Find a survey result you have encountered recently: a poll in the news, a rating on a shopping site, a college feedback summary.

Write down the population the sentence claims to describe, then the sample that was actually measured, then one specific group that is missing from the second but belongs in the first.

For the shopping rating, the population is buyers of that product and the sample is buyers who chose to review, which skews towards the delighted and the furious. Naming that gap takes a minute and changes how you read the number, which is exactly what the rest of this unit is for.
